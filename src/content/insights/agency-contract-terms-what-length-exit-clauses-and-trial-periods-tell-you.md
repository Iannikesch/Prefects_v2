Twelve-month contracts with an early termination fee are common at marketing agencies. Whether yours is reasonable depends on the work, the exit terms and what you can verify early. Here's how to read an agency agreement before you sign.

## When a longer term makes sense

Some work takes time to show its value: brand strategy, a major website rebuild or foundational tracking and attribution setups. An agency that asks for a longer commitment to justify an upfront investment isn't acting in bad faith. The question is whether the ask comes with a clear reason tied to the scope, or is simply presented as standard policy.

## What a shorter term changes

Month-to-month or short terms keep both sides earning the relationship. They give a client room to leave with 30 days' notice if performance slips, and that often shows up in response time and how quickly problems get fixed. The trade-off is that a shorter term can limit the investment an agency is willing to make upfront.

Take an example.A regional retailer signs a twelve-month contract with an agency, discovers within the first quarter that campaign performance is mediocre and communication is slow, but faces a substantial early termination fee that makes leaving financially painful before month six. The retailer stays, frustrated, watching months of underwhelming performance accumulate, because the cost of leaving early outweighs the cost of staying dissatisfied for a while longer. A comparable retailer, working with a different agency on month-to-month terms, notices similar early performance concerns and simply gives notice at the end of month two, moving to a different provider with far less financial friction and far less sunk cost to justify staying somewhere underperforming.

## Read the exit clause

Cancellation terms deserve as much attention as the length of the contract itself, since a technically month-to-month agreement can still contain a lengthy required notice period or a steep off-boarding fee that functions much like a longer lock-in in practice. Reading the actual termination clause, not just the headline contract length, is where the real commitment terms usually live, and it's worth a careful read before assuming a shorter-sounding contract is as flexible as it appears on the surface.

## Ask for a trial period

Negotiating shorter initial terms, even a three-month trial period before committing to anything longer, is a reasonable request many agencies are willing to accommodate, and how an agency responds to that specific ask tends to reveal something useful. How an agency responds is useful information. A flexible answer, or a clear explanation of why the work needs a longer runway, is a good sign. A flat refusal with no reasoning deserves more questions.

## Protect your data and accounts either way

Whatever the length, make sure your contract says that your ad accounts, analytics, pixel history, audiences and customer data belong to you, and that the agency gets access as a partner. You should be able to leave with your data intact and without being tied to specific tools.

## An example

Consider a hypothetical case. A company signs a twelve-month agreement with a 50% early termination fee. By month four, the results are below the agreed targets and the team wants out. The fee, plus the notice period, means leaving costs more than staying another quarter, so it stays and the relationship sours. A different company signs the same twelve months with a 90-day review point and a 30-day notice period after that. At day 90, the numbers are reviewed against targets set in writing. If they're missed, both sides have a clean way out.

The two agreements last the same length. The second one gives both parties a reason to keep performance and communication strong.

## Questions to put in writing

Ask what triggers an early termination fee, whether it declines over time, what the agency hands over when the contract ends and how quickly. Those answers matter more than the headline term.

Finally, keep a short record of what you agreed verbally. Notes from a kickoff call about targets, response times and reporting often differ from the contract's wording. Send a recap email after the call and ask the agency to confirm it, so both sides remember the same agreement.

## The so-what

Before you sign, ask for three things in writing: a three-month trial or review point with a definition of success, a termination clause you've read line by line, and a data and account ownership clause. How an agency handles those requests will tell you more than the pitch deck.

Contract terms vary and this isn't legal advice. Have an attorney review any agreement before you sign.
