A tracking pixel can tell you which ad a person clicked. It can't tell you that they heard your name on a podcast, got a recommendation from a neighbor, or saw your truck parked outside a house down the street. A single question on a form can. 'How did you hear about us?' sounds like a relic from the pre-digital era, and it's one of the most useful fields a business can add.

## What pixels miss

The reason is simple: a lot of what drives demand never leaves a digital trail. Word of mouth, podcasts, community events, a mention in a group chat, a friend's social post. These show up in analytics as 'direct' traffic or as a branded search, which gets credited to whatever channel happened to be last. Self-reported answers fill the gap that tracking structurally can't see.

## Ask the same way every time

Where to ask matters less than asking the same way every time. Put it on the contact or booking form as an optional field, and make it a standard question at phone intake, with the answer logged in the CRM. The key is consistency: the question gets asked the same way each time, and the answer lands in a field somebody can report on.

Open-ended or multiple choice? Both have a place. A free-text box captures answers you wouldn't have thought to offer, such as a specific podcast name or a neighbor's company. A dropdown makes analysis easy but limits people to your guesses. A good compromise is a short dropdown with an 'other, please specify' box, and a quarterly review of what's showing up in 'other'.

## An example

Here's a made-up scenario with invented numbers. A regional law firm that adds the question to its consultation form. Analytics credits 41% of new inquiries to 'direct' and 'organic search.' After three months of self-reported answers, 24% of people say they heard the firm on a local radio show the managing partner appears on, and 17% say a friend or former client referred them. Neither source was visible in analytics. The firm had been about to cut its radio sponsorship because it 'wasn't tracking.'

## Imperfect but useful

The answers aren't perfect, and that's worth knowing. People misremember. 'Google' is a common answer that doesn't distinguish between a paid ad, a map listing, and an organic result. Some respondents pick the first option on the list. Treat self-reported data as a strong directional signal rather than an exact count, and use it alongside tracked data, not instead of it.

Comparing the two is where the insight lives. If analytics says 60% of leads came from paid search and self-reports say 25% mention seeing an ad, the gap isn't a contradiction. It may mean paid search is capturing people who were influenced elsewhere, such as the radio listener who later searched the firm's name and clicked an ad. That's useful to know before cutting the channel that created the demand or doubling the one that harvested it.

## Make it easy for staff

Make it easy for staff. If the front desk has to remember to ask, it won't always happen. Add the question to the intake script, make the CRM field required before a lead can be saved, and give people a short list of standard answers so entries stay consistent. Entering 'FB', 'Facebook', and 'facebook ad' as three separate values makes later reporting painful.

Review the answers monthly. Count the categories, read a sample of the free-text responses, and note anything new. A sudden jump in 'a friend told me' might mean a referral effort is working. A new podcast name appearing repeatedly might point to an unplanned source of leads worth nurturing, or a sponsorship opportunity worth considering.

## Offline businesses benefit most

There's a cultural benefit too. Asking the question reminds everyone that marketing is more than the channels on the dashboard. It credits the work done by satisfied customers and by staff who go the extra mile, and it nudges the business toward investing in those sources deliberately instead of by accident.

A small caution about tone. Keep the question light and optional, and don't use the answers to pressure anyone. The aim is understanding how people found you, not putting them through an interrogation before they book an appointment.

Offline and local businesses benefit most. A contractor, a clinic, or a restaurant often gets customers from signage, vehicle wraps, flyers, and community events that no pixel will ever see. A line on the invoice or booking confirmation, 'How did you find us?', closes that gap. Even a tally on a clipboard at the counter is better than silence.

Coding the answers takes a little discipline. Group free-text replies into categories (referral, radio, search, social, event, other) and keep the category list stable for at least a year, so trends can be compared. Changing categories mid-year makes last quarter's numbers incomparable with this quarter's.

## The so-what

This week, add the question to one intake point, either your main contact form or your phone script, with a short standard list of answers plus an 'other' option. In 60 days, compare what customers say against what your analytics says. The differences will show you where your picture of marketing has been incomplete.
