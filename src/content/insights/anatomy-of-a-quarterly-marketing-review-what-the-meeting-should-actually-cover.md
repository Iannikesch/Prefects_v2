Most quarterly marketing reviews are slide shows. Someone presents a deck full of charts, the room nods, and the meeting ends without a single decision. The data was reviewed but nothing changed, which means the meeting was an expensive way to feel informed. A useful review is structured around decisions instead of presentations.

## Prepare before the meeting

Start before the meeting. Circulate the numbers at least a day ahead, so the meeting doesn't spend its first half reading slides aloud. Include results against targets, spend by channel, key funnel metrics, and a short list of questions the data raises. People who've read the material can use the meeting to discuss it.

## Results, funnel and learning

Open with results against the goals set last quarter. Not general performance, but the specific targets agreed in advance. Did leads, revenue, and cost per customer land where they were supposed to? If targets were missed, say so plainly and move quickly to why. A review that avoids misses isn't a review.

Next, walk through the funnel, not just the top of it. Ad performance is only the beginning. Look at leads, qualified leads, conversations, bookings, and revenue, and find where the biggest losses occurred. Many quarters reveal that the biggest problem wasn't marketing's reach but what happened to the leads afterward.

Then discuss what was learned. Which tests were run, what the results were, and what they suggest. Include the tests that found nothing, since knowing what doesn't matter is valuable. If nobody ran any tests, that's a finding too, and it should lead to a plan for next quarter.

## Spend, measurement and risks

Review spend efficiency with an eye to the margin, not just the average. Which channels are producing customers at a cost that still pays back, and which have drifted past the point of diminishing returns? This is the place to decide whether to move money, using small, tested shifts instead of big swings.

Check the health of the measurement itself. Are conversions tracking correctly? Do platform numbers reconcile with the CRM and the accounting system within a reasonable margin? A short audit each quarter prevents slow drift, and it protects every decision made in the meeting.

Cover risks and dependencies. Is the business too reliant on one channel? Has a platform changed its rules? Are any contracts coming up for renewal? Is anyone key about to leave? A quarterly review is a natural place to surface problems while there's still time to deal with them.

## An example

Consider a made-up case. A company whose old review was a 45-slide deck presented by its agency, with no decisions recorded. After restructuring, the meeting runs 75 minutes with a one-page pre-read, and ends with a list of decisions: shift 10% of display spend to search for eight weeks, fix a form that's dropping 12% of submissions, and run two landing page tests. A month later, everyone remembers what was agreed, because it was written down.

## Decisions and owners

End with decisions and owners. Every item that's agreed should have a name, a date, and a way of checking whether it happened. Record them in a shared document, and begin the next review by going through the list. Without this, the same issues appear again and again, as if the meeting had a short memory.

Keep the room small and the time bounded. The people who make decisions about budgets, offers, and operations should attend, along with whoever manages the channels. A meeting of fifteen people tends to produce presentations, while one of five tends to produce decisions. An hour to ninety minutes is enough if the preparation is done.

Write a one-paragraph summary for people who weren't there. Leadership and other departments rarely need the full detail, but they do need to know what the results were, what's changing, and what help is needed. A short written summary sent the same day keeps the review from living and dying inside one room.

Separate review from planning when the agenda gets crowded. A meeting that tries to examine last quarter and design the next one in the same hour usually does both badly. Many teams hold the review first and a short planning session a few days later, so decisions about next quarter benefit from time to think about what the numbers showed.

Bring in the people who touch customers after the lead arrives. Hearing from sales or operations what the leads looked like, which objections came up, and what customers asked for gives the numbers a human context. Marketing teams that talk only to each other tend to misread their own data.

Keep a running list of questions that couldn't be answered. Each quarter, note the things the data couldn't tell you, such as why a segment dropped or what drove a spike. Those gaps become the next quarter's measurement projects, and over time they push the business toward the data it really needs.

## The so-what

Reviews that end in decisions build on one another. Over a year, the habit of setting targets, testing, checking, and recording what changed gives a business something most competitors lack: a documented memory of what works for its own customers. That record becomes more valuable with each quarter, and it makes the next budget conversation far shorter.
