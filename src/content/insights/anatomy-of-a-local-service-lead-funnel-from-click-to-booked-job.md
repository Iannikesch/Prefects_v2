For a local service business, the real funnel doesn't end at the lead. It runs from an ad click to a phone call or form, through a conversation, an estimate, a booking, and finally a completed job. Marketing often gets judged at the first step, while the money is decided at the last. This walk through an illustrative funnel, with made-up numbers, shows where results disappear.

## Start with the contacts

Picture a plumbing company in a mid-sized Texas suburb spending $6,000 a month on search and local service ads. In an average month, the ads generate 120 contacts: 78 phone calls and 42 form submissions. That's $50 per contact, and that number goes into the monthly report.

Now follow those 120. Nine of the calls are wrong numbers or solicitations, and 11 of the form submissions are spam or from outside the service area. That leaves 100 real opportunities, and the cost per real opportunity is $60, not $50. Already the report is a little rosier than the truth.

## Where the callers go

Of the 69 real calls, 14 arrive after hours and go to voicemail. Only 5 of those get a callback within an hour. The other 9 customers, in all likelihood, called a competitor. So 91 opportunities turn into an actual conversation.

Of those 91 conversations, 52 become scheduled estimates, a rate of 57%. Estimate conversion is a sales skill, not a marketing one, but marketing should still track it. Where do the other 39 go? Are people asking for prices over the phone and hanging up? Is the intake script losing them? Listening to a sample of recorded calls (with proper disclosure) usually shows patterns within an hour.

Of 52 estimates, 29 become booked jobs, a rate of 56%. At an average ticket of $640, that's $18,560 in revenue on $6,000 of spend, a return of about 3.1 to 1, or roughly $207 per booked job. That's the number the business actually runs on, and it only appears at the bottom of the funnel.

## Fix the leaks

Each step leaks, and the biggest controllable losses aren't advertising problems. The nine callers who never reached anyone and the gap between conversations and estimates are operations problems that show up as marketing underperformance. Speed matters most. People searching for a plumber usually call several companies, and the first to answer often wins. A call-forwarding setup that rings a mobile after hours, or an automatic text that replies within a minute to a missed call, can recover a good share of that loss at almost no cost.

Booking rate depends on follow-up. Estimates that get no response within two days tend to go cold. A same-day text and a call two days later is more than many small businesses do. In this composite funnel, lifting the booking rate from 56% to 62% adds about three jobs a month, roughly $1,900 in revenue, without spending another ad dollar.

Seasonality changes the shape of the funnel. During a heat wave or a freeze, call volume spikes and the front desk gets overwhelmed, which means the same business that answers 85% of calls in a normal week may answer far fewer at the moment demand peaks. Staffing for peaks, even temporarily, can matter more than any ad optimization done that month.

## Close the loop and report it

The job itself feeds back into marketing. A finished job should trigger a review request within a day or two, since reviews improve local ad performance and organic visibility. Satisfied customers can also be asked for referrals. The funnel loops back on itself: the end of one customer's journey helps start the next.

Reporting should mirror the funnel. A monthly view showing contacts, real opportunities, conversations, estimates, booked jobs, and revenue lets everyone see where results drop and who owns the fix. Marketing owns the first stage. Operations and sales own the rest. When every stage sits in one report, finger-pointing gives way to a shared to-do list.

Attribution makes this funnel harder to read than it looks. A homeowner might click a local service ad, call, hang up, and then search the company's name two days later before booking. Without call tracking and a CRM that ties the booking to the first contact, that job gets credited to branded search or to no one. Tie each booked job to its original source, even imperfectly, or the funnel numbers will understate what the ads produced.

Average ticket varies by lead source in ways worth checking. In this hypothetical, emergency calls from search ads average $410, while estimate requests from the website form average $1,100 because they're planned projects. A cost per booked job that looks high for form leads may be perfectly healthy once the ticket size is considered. Looking at revenue by source stops the business from cutting its most valuable lead type because the cost per lead looked high.

## The so-what

Pick the weakest stage in your own funnel and fix it before you buy more clicks. If you can't measure each stage yet, start by tracking calls and tying each booked job back to its original source.
