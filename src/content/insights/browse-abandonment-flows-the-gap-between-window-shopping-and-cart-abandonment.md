Cart abandonment gets all the attention, and for good reason: someone who adds an item to a cart is close to buying. But most visitors never get that far. They look at a few products, maybe the same one twice, and leave. That large group of window shoppers sits in a gap that a browse abandonment flow is built to fill.

## The gap before the cart

A browse abandonment flow sends a message to a visitor who viewed a product or category but didn't add anything to a cart. It only works when the visitor can be identified, which usually means they're a subscriber, a logged-in customer, or someone who previously clicked through from an email. Anonymous visitors can't receive a message, no matter how interested they were.

The intent is weaker than in a cart abandonment, and the message should reflect that. Someone who added an item and left has made a decision that was interrupted. Someone who looked at a product was curious. Treating both the same, with an aggressive discount sent within the hour, mistakes curiosity for commitment and gives away margin for nothing.

## Keep it gentle

A gentler approach tends to work better. A reminder of the item viewed, a few related products, perhaps a short note on what makes it worth considering, such as reviews or a size guide. The message helps the person continue their research instead of pushing them to check out, and it keeps the brand in mind without training anyone to wait for a coupon.

Timing sits somewhere between an hour and a day after the browse. Too soon and the message feels like surveillance. Too late and the moment has passed. Test a window, though. A business with impulse-priced products might do better on the early end, while one selling considered purchases may need a longer gap.

## An example

Consider a hypothetical case. A footwear retailer whose site has about 14,000 identified visitors browsing in a month. Its browse abandonment flow sends roughly 6,200 emails to those who viewed a product page without adding to cart. In this hypothetical, the flow produces 41 orders at an average of $78, or about $3,200 in revenue a month, from a flow that took a few hours to build and sends no discount at all. That isn't huge, and it doesn't need to be. It's revenue the store wasn't collecting.

## Coordinate and cap it

Coordinate with the cart flow. A shopper who browses, then adds to cart, shouldn't keep receiving browse messages. Set rules so that adding to cart exits the browse flow and enters the cart flow, and so that a purchase exits both. Sending three overlapping sequences to the same person in one day is a fast route to unsubscribes.

Frequency caps matter. A customer who browses ten products in a week doesn't need ten emails. Limit the flow to one message per browsing session or per few days, and consider suppressing it for people who received another marketing email in the past day. The flow should feel occasional, not constant.

## Consent first

Consent and privacy come first. Tying browsing behavior to an identified person depends on the tracking and consent the business has in place. Make sure the privacy policy and cookie notice describe this use, honor opt-outs, and don't apply the flow to people who haven't agreed to receive marketing. Check how your jurisdiction treats behavioral tracking and consult counsel if unsure.

Personalization helps when it's accurate and hurts when it's wrong. Showing the product someone viewed is a plus. Showing a product they already bought is awkward. Make sure the flow excludes recent purchasers and updates as inventory changes, since an email promoting something that's sold out wastes the goodwill the message was meant to build.

## Measure incrementality

Judge the flow on incremental revenue, not on attributed revenue alone. Some of the people who receive the email would have come back and bought anyway. A holdback group, with a small slice of eligible visitors deliberately not receiving the flow, shows how many additional orders the emails create. It takes a few weeks of data to read, but it keeps the flow honest.

Segment by what was viewed. A shopper who browsed running shoes needs a different message from one who browsed formal ones. Where product category data is available, build separate versions or use dynamic content that adapts to the category. Relevance is the whole point of this flow, and a generic 'we saw you looking' misses it.

Mobile matters. Many browsing sessions happen on phones, and the email is likely to be read there. Check that product images load quickly, the call to action is easy to tap, and the link lands on the product page, not the homepage. Every extra tap loses someone, and these shoppers weren't committed to begin with.

## The so-what

Check whether your email platform can trigger a message from product page views. If it can, build a single, discount-free browse flow this week with one email, a clear product reminder, and an exit for cart adds and purchases. Let it run for a month, then compare against a holdback before deciding whether it deserves a second email.
