The first month with a new agency sets the pattern for everything that follows. Done well, it produces a clear baseline, working tracking, and a shared definition of success. Done badly, it produces a flurry of activity, a polished kickoff deck, and nobody able to say, six months later, what the starting point was.

## Week one: access and baseline

Week one should be about access and ownership, not strategy. Who owns the ad accounts, the analytics property, the CRM, the domain? Make sure the business is the account owner or admin on all of them, with the agency granted access as a partner. This is the single easiest moment to get that right. Asking later feels awkward. Asking now is just part of the checklist.

Week one should also produce a documented baseline. Before any change is made, capture current performance: spend, leads, cost per lead, conversion rates, revenue by channel, whatever applies. Take screenshots and export the data. Without a baseline, any later claim of improvement is a matter of memory. With one, it's arithmetic.

## Fix tracking before spending

Tracking comes next. Before launching new campaigns, the agency should audit what's already measured and what isn't. Are conversions firing correctly? Is the CRM connected? Are phone calls tracked? An agency that starts spending before it has checked the tracking is building on an unknown foundation, and any early results will be hard to trust.

## Define success in numbers

Weeks two and three should define success in specific terms. Not 'grow awareness' or 'improve ROI', but numbers. A target cost per lead. A target return on ad spend. Acceptable ranges and a timeline for when each is expected. The agency may need a few weeks of data before committing to numbers, and that's reasonable, but the conversation about how targets will be set should happen now.

## An example

Take an example with made-up numbers. A home services company that onboards a new agency in the third week of March. The agency spends the first ten days getting access, documenting last quarter's results (a cost per lead of $87 and a booked-job rate of 22%), and fixing a form tag that had been double counting. Only then does it launch two test campaigns. When the first monthly report arrives, the owner can compare it to a baseline everyone agreed was accurate, and the discussion is about real changes instead of competing recollections.

## It runs both directions

Onboarding runs both directions. The agency needs things from the business too: brand assets, product or service details, pricing, a list of past promotions that worked or flopped, and, most of all, a named decision maker who can approve things quickly. Agencies stall for days waiting on feedback from someone who's too busy. If you're slow to respond in month one, expect slow results to follow.

Communication rhythm should be established early. Who's the day-to-day contact? How often are meetings held? What does the weekly update include, and in what format? Agree on how quickly questions get answered. These sound like administrative details, but they decide whether problems are caught in days or discovered at the quarterly review.

Ask who will actually be working on the account, by name and role, and how much time each person will spend on it. Confirm that the people from the pitch are the people doing the work, or learn who replaced them. If the answer is vague now, it will be vaguer after the contract is a few months old.

## Set expectations for month one

Expect the first 30 days to be mostly setup and early tests, not dramatic results. Be wary of an agency promising big wins in the first two weeks, since quick gains often come from short-term tactics that don't last. A good first month ends with clean data, an agreed plan, and a few early signals. Results usually come after that.

Ask for a 30-day plan in writing. It should list what will be done each week, who is responsible, and what the business needs to provide. A plan on paper turns vague promises into commitments that can be checked. When day 20 arrives and a deliverable is missing, there's no argument about what was agreed. It also tells you how organized the agency really is, which is information you'd otherwise only get by waiting.

Set a first review at day 30 and a deeper one at day 90. The 30-day meeting checks the foundations: access, tracking, baseline, goals, communication. The 90-day meeting is the first honest look at performance against the targets set in week three. Having both dates on the calendar from the start keeps the relationship focused on evidence and prevents it from drifting into a monthly slide show with no consequences.

Finally, keep a record of what changes the agency makes. A simple change log, with the date, the change, and the reason, helps connect later shifts in performance to what caused them. Without one, a good month and a bad month both turn into guesses about what happened.

## The so-what

If you're about to start with an agency, or you're in month one now, write a one-page checklist covering account ownership, baseline numbers, tracking verification, and agreed targets. Ask the agency to confirm each item in writing by day 30. It takes an hour to draft, and it will make every conversation afterward easier.

Contract terms vary and this isn't legal advice. Have an attorney review any agreement before you sign.
