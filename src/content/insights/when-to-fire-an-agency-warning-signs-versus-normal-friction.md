Every agency relationship hits rough patches. A slow month, a creative disagreement, an awkward report. Most of them are ordinary friction and don't mean the relationship is failing. A few are real warning signs. The difficulty is telling them apart before frustration makes the decision for you.

## What's normal

Start with what's normal. The first 60 to 90 days of any engagement are often bumpy, because the agency is still learning the business and tracking is still being cleaned up. A single bad month, especially for a business with modest volume, is often noise. Disagreement over creative direction is routine. None of these alone is grounds for a break.

## Warning signs

Now the warning signs. The first is an inability to explain results. If the agency can't say clearly why numbers moved, or answers questions with jargon instead of specifics, either they don't know or they don't want to say. Neither is acceptable. A good account team can explain a result in plain language and say what they'll try next.

The second is shifting goalposts. Targets set at the start quietly change. 'Leads' becomes 'engagement'. A missed cost-per-lead goal gets explained by a new metric that looks better. When the definition of success changes whenever results disappoint, the relationship isn't being measured.

A third is defensiveness about access and data. If asking for admin access to your own ad accounts, or a copy of your reports, produces hesitation or delay, treat that as significant. The business should own its data and accounts, and a confident agency has no reason to resist.

## An example

Here's a hypothetical. A regional retailer five months into an agency relationship. Monthly reports have shifted from showing sales to showing impressions, the account manager has changed twice, and a request for read access to the ad account has been 'in progress' for three weeks. Each of these might be explained individually. Together they form a pattern. The retailer responds by requesting access in writing with a deadline, asking for a full performance summary against the original targets, and quietly documenting what a transition would require.

Silence is worth noticing as well. An agency that goes quiet when results are poor, and communicates loudly only when results are good, is managing the relationship instead of the account. Good partners bring bad news early, with a plan attached. If you're repeatedly the one who has to ask what happened, that says something about how the agency sees its job.

## Before you end it

Before ending a relationship, do the groundwork. Review the contract for notice periods and termination terms. Confirm which accounts and assets the business already controls. Save exports of historical data and creative. If access to anything sits under the agency's name, plan how to transfer it. A decision made calmly takes a week to prepare. A decision made in anger often costs months of lost data.

Try a direct conversation first, unless the warning signs are severe. Describe the specific problems, point to the original goals, and ask for a written plan to correct course within 30 days. Good agencies respond to this by getting more specific and more engaged. Poor ones respond with excuses or silence, which also tells you what you needed to know.

Also consider your own part. Slow approvals, shifting priorities, and unclear goals can sink an agency relationship as surely as bad execution. Check whether you've given the agency what it needs. An agency that was set up to fail isn't evidence about the agency, and switching won't fix a problem that follows you to the next one.

Keep a running log of issues. A short document noting dates, what was promised, what was delivered, and what you asked about makes the decision far clearer. Patterns that feel vague in the moment become obvious on paper. Three missed deadlines in two months looks different when it's written in a list. And if the relationship does end, the log protects you in any dispute over what was agreed.

Consider the cost of switching. A new agency means a new onboarding period, new tracking checks, and a stretch of reduced performance while it learns the account. If the current agency's problems are fixable, and they're willing to fix them, staying may beat starting over. If the problems are structural, such as poor strategy or no accountability, the cost of switching is usually lower than the cost of another year of the same.

Get a second opinion before deciding. A one-time review by an independent consultant, or a quick audit of the accounts by someone with no stake in the outcome, can confirm whether the performance is as weak as it feels. Sometimes it reveals the agency has been doing reasonable work in a hard market. Sometimes it reveals problems nobody had seen.

## The so-what

So ask yourself: if the agency disappeared tomorrow, could you explain what they've achieved, show the numbers behind it, and access every account they run? If not, the problem may not be that you should leave. It may be that you've never had a clear enough view to judge whether you should stay.

Contract terms vary and this isn't legal advice. Have an attorney review any agreement and your termination rights before ending a relationship.
