Ask a business owner how much they can spend to acquire a customer, and the answer is usually a feeling. 'About fifty bucks, I guess.' Sometimes it's whatever the last campaign happened to cost. The better answer comes from a number most small businesses have never calculated: what a customer is actually worth over time.

## What LTV is

Customer lifetime value, or LTV, is the total profit a typical customer brings in across the whole relationship. The simplest version multiplies three things: average order value, how many orders a customer places per year, and how many years they stick around. Then you take the share that's actually profit, meaning gross margin, rather than treating all revenue as if it were free money.

That last step is where many calculations go wrong. A customer who spends $1,000 on products with a 30% margin is worth $300, not $1,000. Spending $400 to acquire them looks fine against the revenue figure and loses money against the margin. If you only take one habit from this post, make it this one: do the math on profit.

## An example

Consider a made-up case. A subscription pet-supply company. Average order is $42. A typical customer orders 5.5 times a year and stays about 2.4 years. Gross margin is 55%. Multiply it out and a customer is worth roughly $305 in gross profit over their lifetime. If it costs $90 to acquire that customer, the ratio is a little better than 3 to 1, and the business recoups its acquisition cost in about eight and a half months of margin.

## Ratio and payback

A ratio of about 3 to 1 gets quoted as a healthy target in a lot of circles, but treat it as a rule of thumb rather than a law. A business with strong cash reserves and a long horizon might happily run at 2 to 1 to grow faster. A business with thin cash needs a faster payback, because it can't wait two years to be repaid. The right threshold depends on how long you can afford to wait.

Payback period deserves as much attention as the ratio. It answers a different question: how long until a new customer has returned what you spent to get them? A 4 to 1 ratio that takes three years to materialize can strangle a company that needs cash this quarter. Looking at both numbers together keeps growth from outrunning the bank account.

## Compare channels

LTV changes the conversation about channels. In this example, paid social brings in customers at $120 each, and referrals bring them in at $35. On cost alone, referrals win easily. But if referred customers also stay longer, say 3.1 years instead of 2.0, the gap widens further. And a channel that looks expensive on cost per acquisition can still be worth running if its customers are worth far more. You can only see that by tracking value by source.

Averages hide a lot. A single blended LTV for the whole customer base can mask the fact that one group is worth three times another. When enough data exists, calculate LTV by acquisition channel and by first product purchased, and compare them. The differences often change where the next dollar of budget goes.

## Be conservative

New businesses face a data problem. With six months of history, you can't know how long customers stay. In that case, use early indicators as a stand-in, such as the margin from the first 90 days and the share who place a second order. Treat the estimate as provisional and update it as the data matures. A rough LTV built from real early behavior beats a confident guess built from nothing.

Resist the temptation to inflate. It's tempting to assume customers will stay longer than they have, or to count revenue that hasn't happened yet. Optimistic LTV leads to optimistic ad budgets, and the shortfall shows up in cash flow, not in a spreadsheet. If in doubt, use the conservative number, and let reality pleasantly surprise you.

Finally, share the number. LTV is only useful if the people buying ads, setting offers, and deciding on discounts all know it. A target acquisition cost derived from LTV gives the team a concrete ceiling to work under, and it stops arguments that start with 'this campaign feels expensive' and end with nothing resolved.

A quick sanity check helps before relying on the figure. Compare the LTV you calculated with what you can see in actual customer history. Pick customers acquired two or three years ago and add up the profit they've really generated. If the real number is far below the formula's estimate, the retention or frequency assumptions are too generous. Real cohorts beat formulas, and a small audit like this takes an afternoon.

Discounts and returns belong in the calculation. Revenue figures that ignore refunds, promotional discounts, shipping subsidies, and payment processing fees overstate what each customer is worth. Subtract them before applying margin, or use contribution margin where the data allows. The result is a less flattering number, and a more useful one.

## The so-what

This week, calculate LTV from profit, not revenue, for your top acquisition channel. Add the payback period, set a target acquisition cost under that ceiling and share it with whoever buys the ads.
