Some products run out. Coffee, pet food, contact lenses, skincare, printer ink, vitamins. For these, the most valuable email a business can send is the one that arrives just before the customer runs out, when the need is real and the decision is easy. Most businesses send a generic 'we miss you' message weeks later, or nothing at all.

## Send before they run out

The idea is to work out how long a typical purchase lasts and send a reminder shortly before it's used up. A 12-ounce bag of coffee might last a two-cup-a-day household about two weeks. A 90-day supply of vitamins lasts, well, 90 days. The reminder lands when the customer is already thinking about the next order, which is why these messages tend to convert far better than a promotional blast.

Don't guess the interval. Pull your own data. For each product, calculate the median number of days between a customer's first and second order. If the median for a particular moisturizer is 52 days, then a reminder at day 42 to 45 catches people before they run out. Medians are more reliable than averages here, since a few very late reorders can drag an average well past what most people do.

## An example

Consider a made-up case. A coffee roaster that studies its reorder data and finds that customers buying the 12-ounce bag typically reorder after 17 days, while those buying the 2-pound bag reorder after about 41 days. It builds two timing rules: a reminder at day 13 for the small bag and at day 34 for the large one. In this hypothetical, the reorder rate within five days of the reminder is 19%, versus 8% for the previous practice of a single reminder at day 30 for everyone.

## Personalize the interval

Zero-party data can sharpen the timing. If a customer tells you how many people in the household drink coffee, or how often their dog eats, you can set an interval that fits them rather than the median. A household of four finishes a bag faster than a single person, and a reminder that ignores that will be wrong for both.

## Make reordering effortless

Make reordering effortless. A reminder that links directly to the customer's last order, with a one-click reorder button, removes steps and doubles as a convenience. Offering a subscription option at that point, with delivery on a schedule that matches their usage, turns a recurring reminder into a recurring order, and it gives customers who dislike email a way to avoid it.

Be careful with discounts. A customer who is about to run out and wants the product doesn't need a coupon to reorder. Offering one trains them to wait for it and gives away margin on a sale that was likely anyway. Reserve incentives for customers who have passed the expected reorder date by a meaningful margin, when a nudge might be what's needed.

## Know the exceptions

Not every purchase is a candidate. A gift, a one-time trial of a new product, or an item bought for a project doesn't follow the pattern. Flag first-time buyers separately and consider waiting for a second purchase before assuming a rhythm. Reminding someone to buy another set of garden tools because they bought one last month is the quickest way to look like you don't understand your own products.

SMS can work well here, since the reminder is timely, relevant, and requested by the nature of the relationship. Where the customer has opted in to texts, a short message with a reorder link tends to be welcome. Where they haven't, email is the proper channel. Either way, send only when the product is likely to be running low.

Suppress the reminder if the customer already reordered. Few things look sloppier than a 'time to restock' email arriving the day after an order. Connect the flow to order data so that any purchase of the product, in any channel, cancels the reminder. Phone orders and in-store purchases are easy to forget, so confirm those reach the same system.

Review the results by product. Some items will respond strongly to reminders and others barely at all. Where a product has a long and irregular usage pattern, the reminder may not be worth the effort. Where the pattern is tight, as with a consumable used daily, it's worth testing the timing in small steps, a day earlier or later, to find the sweet spot.

Handle the exceptions. A customer who has paused deliveries, cancelled a subscription, or recently contacted support with a complaint shouldn't receive an upbeat reminder to reorder. Build suppression rules from those signals, so the flow stays out of situations where it would be tone-deaf. A well-timed message to the wrong person is still the wrong message.

Seasonal products need adjustments. A sunscreen or a heating filter follows the calendar as much as the usage rate, so the median interval may be misleading. Look at the timing by season, and set the reminder accordingly.

## The so-what

In short, a replenishment reminder works because it arrives when the customer's need does. Base the timing on real reorder data, tailor it with what customers tell you, make the reorder a single click, and keep discounts out of the way unless the customer has gone quiet. It's one of the simplest ways to turn a single purchase into a habit.
