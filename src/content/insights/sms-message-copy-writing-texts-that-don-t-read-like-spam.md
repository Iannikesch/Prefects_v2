A text message has about one sentence to work with. There's no subject line, no header image, no space for a long pitch. That constraint is a gift for businesses that know what they want to say, and a trap for those that don't. The difference between a text that gets read and one that gets deleted often comes down to a few choices in the copy.

## Say who you are

Start by saying who you are. A message from an unfamiliar number with no brand name looks like a scam, and it can read as one even when the person did sign up. Open with the business name: 'Hi, it's [Business name].' People who forgot they subscribed need that anchor, and it builds trust for the ones who remember.

Say something worth reading. The test for any promotional text is whether the recipient would be glad to have received it. A restock alert for something they wanted passes. A generic 'BIG SALE!!!' with no detail doesn't. The more specific the value, the less the message feels like noise: what's on offer, for whom, until when.

## One message, one action

One message, one action. A text that includes a discount, a new product announcement, a survey link, and a reminder about store hours leaves the reader with four things to ignore. Choose the single thing you want them to do, put it at the end, and make it obvious. Short links or a clear reply keyword work better than a paragraph of instructions.

## Mind the length and links

Watch the length. A standard text segment holds 160 characters of basic text, and messages that contain emoji or certain special characters drop to 70 characters per segment. Longer messages get split and billed as multiple segments, and some phones display them awkwardly. That's not a reason to cram, but it is a reason to edit.

Be careful with links. Carriers tend to filter messages containing public link-shortening services, which are heavily used by spammers, so a message that might have been delivered can quietly vanish. A branded short domain, or a full link to your own site, usually fares better. Ask your texting platform what it recommends and test delivery to a few real phones.

## Match the tone

Tone should match the relationship. A text lands in a personal space, so a friendly, plain voice works better than a corporate one. Avoid ALL CAPS, strings of exclamation points, and walls of emoji. A little personality is welcome. Pretending to be a close friend ('Hey girl!!') to a stranger who bought once is not.

## Include the required elements

Include the legally required elements. In many jurisdictions, promotional texts must identify the sender and give a way to opt out, and your carrier or platform may require specific language such as 'Reply STOP to opt out.' Requirements vary, and this isn't legal advice, so confirm the current rules for your situation with someone who is. Leaving them out isn't a copywriting choice. It's a risk.

## An example

Here's a made-up scenario. A home goods retailer testing two versions of the same promotion over four sends. Version A: 'FLASH SALE!! 30% off everything TODAY ONLY!! Click now: [short link].' Version B: 'Hi, it's [Business name]. Our linen sale ends tonight at midnight. 30% off sheets and towels, no code needed: [full link]. Reply STOP to opt out.' In this hypothetical, B gets 2.4 times the click rate and half the opt-outs. The offer was identical. The difference was clarity, a named sender, and a message that sounded like a person.

## Timing, testing and replies

Timing and frequency are part of the copy. A great message at 9 p.m. on a Sunday may still annoy. Send during reasonable hours for the recipient's time zone, and keep the volume low enough that each message feels like it matters. A shorter, rarer program tends to retain subscribers better than a daily drip.

Test one thing at a time: the opening line, the offer wording, the call to action. Judge on clicks and revenue per message, and on opt-out rate as the check against being too aggressive. A message that wins on clicks but doubles unsubscribes may be borrowing against the list's future.

Consider the reply experience. If a customer texts back a question rather than a keyword, someone has to see it. A promotional text that invites replies and then sends nothing back feels broken. Decide before sending whether replies will be monitored, and say so in the opt-in language if they won't be.

Personalization needs care. Using a first name is common, but a wrong or missing name ('Hi ,') is worse than none. Check that the field exists for most subscribers, set a fallback, and consider whether personalization based on past purchases adds value or just feels watchful. The line between helpful and creepy is thinner by text than by email.

## The so-what

Pull up the last three promotional texts your business sent. For each, check that the sender is named, there's one clear action, the link is trustworthy, and the opt-out language is present. Rewrite the weakest one using those four checks, and send it as a test against the original.
