A subscriber who hasn't opened an email in eight months is sitting in most businesses' lists as dead weight, and the instinct is almost always the same: send a re-engagement campaign, usually built around a discount, to try to win them back before giving up entirely. That instinct is right often enough to be worth acting on, but it's wrong often enough that treating it as an automatic rule, applied to every dormant subscriber identically, wastes effort on people who were never coming back and, worse, actively damages deliverability for the subscribers who were.

## The case for trying

The case for attempting re-engagement is simple: a dormant subscriber who once opted in has already demonstrated some initial interest, and reactivating that relationship is typically far cheaper than acquiring a brand-new customer from scratch through paid advertising. A well-targeted re-engagement email, sent to someone who's simply drifted rather than actively disengaged, can bring back a meaningful share of a dormant segment. Results vary widely by industry and list quality, so treat any benchmark as a starting point and measure your own.

## The case for letting go

The case against attempting it, or at least against attempting it indefinitely, comes down to what happens to the subscribers who don't respond. Continuing to send regular campaign emails to someone who hasn't opened anything in over a year drags down a business's overall engagement rate, which mail providers use as a key signal when deciding whether to route future emails to an inbox or to spam. A large dormant segment sitting in an active sending list isn't neutral. It's actively working against deliverability for every other subscriber on that same list.

## An example

Consider a made-up case. A subscription retailer identifies 22,000 subscribers who haven't opened an email in over nine months, out of a total list of 140,000. Running a single, well-crafted re-engagement campaign to this segment brings back roughly 2,400 subscribers who click through and re-engage with at least one subsequent email. The remaining 19,600 don't respond at all. Suppressing that non-responsive group from future regular sends, rather than continuing to include them indefinitely, produces a measurable lift in the retailer's overall open and deliverability metrics within the following two sending cycles, since the dead weight dragging down the aggregate engagement rate has been removed.

## Run a real sequence

Building a genuine re-engagement sequence, rather than a single generic email, tends to produce better results before a business commits to suppression. A short series, typically two to three emails over a couple of weeks, starting with a simple we miss you message before escalating to a stronger incentive in a final attempt, gives a dormant subscriber multiple chances to respond before a business concludes the relationship is over. A single email, sent once and never followed up on, undersells how much a dormant subscriber might actually respond to a slightly more persistent, well-sequenced attempt.

The decision to finally suppress or remove a non-responsive subscriber after a re-engagement sequence fails shouldn't feel like a loss, worth reframing directly. A list that shrinks because disengaged subscribers get removed is a healthier list, not a smaller failure, since every remaining subscriber is now someone more likely to actually engage with future sends, which in turn improves deliverability for the entire remaining list going forward.

## Win-back is different

This differs from a standard win-back flow in an important way worth distinguishing clearly. A win-back sequence typically targets a customer who's stopped purchasing but may still be opening emails. A re-engagement campaign targets someone who's stopped opening entirely, a deeper level of disengagement that requires a different kind of message and, eventually, a different kind of decision about whether to keep sending to them at all.

## Segment and time it

Segmenting a dormant list before attempting re-engagement, rather than treating every non-opener identically, tends to improve results meaningfully. A subscriber who hasn't opened anything in nine months but previously purchased multiple times represents a different opportunity than one who never purchased at all and only ever opened a single welcome email before going quiet, and a single generic message aimed at both groups undersells the stronger case that exists for trying harder with the former.

Timing the initial re-engagement attempt matters too, and waiting too long past the point of genuine disengagement reduces the odds of a successful save. A subscriber flagged as dormant the moment they cross a defined inactivity threshold, rather than left sitting unaddressed for several additional months, is generally easier to win back than one who's drifted for a much longer stretch, since the relationship, however faint, is still fresher and easier to reactivate the sooner it gets addressed.

## The so-what

Run re-engagement once, as a defined and time-limited attempt: segment by past purchase history, send two or three emails over a couple of weeks, then suppress anyone who doesn't respond. A smaller list of people who read your email is worth more than a larger one that drags down your deliverability.
