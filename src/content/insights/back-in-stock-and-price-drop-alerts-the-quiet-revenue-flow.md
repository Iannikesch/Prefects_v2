Some of the highest-intent shoppers on a website are the ones who can't buy anything. They landed on a product page, wanted it, and found it sold out. Most stores let that moment evaporate. A back-in-stock alert turns it into a sign-up, and later, into a sale.

## Intent you can't buy elsewhere

The mechanism is simple. On a sold-out product page, a button asks for an email address or phone number to be notified when the item returns. When inventory refills, an automated message goes out. There's no discount involved and no persuasion required. The customer asked for the message. That's about as warm as marketing gets.

Why does it work? These shoppers have already done the hard part. They've found the product, decided they want it, and told you so. Compare that with a cold prospect who needs convincing of everything. These alerts often outperform typical promotional emails because the audience selected itself, though results vary by product and list.

## A free demand signal

The list is also a free demand signal. If 340 people signed up to be notified about one jacket in a size medium, that's information a buyer would love to have before placing the next purchase order. Merchandising teams often never see it, because the data sits in a marketing tool and nobody forwards it. A simple monthly report of the most waitlisted items can change what gets reordered.

## An example

Here's a made-up scenario with invented numbers. A small outdoor gear retailer with a popular sleeping bag that sells out every spring. Before alerts, shoppers who found it unavailable simply left. After adding a notify-me button, the store collects 212 sign-ups during a six-week stockout. When 150 units arrive, an alert goes out and about 40% of the list buys within three days, mostly before the item gets promoted anywhere else. That's roughly 85 sales from a flow that took one afternoon to set up.

## Price-drop alerts

Price-drop alerts work on the same principle for a different hesitation. A shopper who wants an item but not at that price can ask to be told if it falls. When it does, the message goes to someone who has said, explicitly, that price was the obstacle. Contrast that with a sitewide sale email sent to everyone, which hands discounts to people who would have paid full price.

## Get the details right

A few things to get right. Be honest about timing: if the item returns in limited quantity, say so, since nothing sours a customer faster than an alert for something that's gone again by the time they click. Send alerts quickly after restock, ideally within the hour. And don't treat the sign-up as general marketing consent unless the form said so. Someone who requested one alert hasn't necessarily agreed to a weekly newsletter.

SMS suits this flow well, since speed matters and the customer has already asked for that specific message. Where the sign-up covered text alerts for restocks, a short text with a direct product link is hard to beat. Where it didn't, email is the safer channel, and the opt-in language should be clear about which one the customer chose.

Setup is usually simple on modern ecommerce platforms. Many offer a built-in back-in-stock feature, and several apps add more control, including variant-level alerts (the medium in blue, not just the jacket). Variant-level matters, since a customer who wants a size small isn't interested in hearing that the extra-large is back.

## Pick the right products

Not every product earns the effort. Focus on items with real, repeat demand: seasonal bestsellers, limited runs, and anything that regularly sells out. A slow-moving item that happens to be out of stock mostly signals a purchasing decision, not a missed opportunity. Look at which sold-out pages draw the most traffic, since that's the clearest sign of unmet demand.

Track the alert as its own flow. Measure sign-ups per out-of-stock page, open and click rates, conversion on the alert, and the time between alert and purchase. Also look at how many sign-ups never convert. If a large share never buy, the alerts may be reaching people who've moved on, and a reminder a week later can recover some of them.

A related opportunity: when an item is out of stock, offer an alternative on the same page. A customer who wanted the green jacket might happily take the olive one. Pairing a notify-me button with a few similar in-stock items captures people who need something now, while still collecting a list for the restock.

One caution is inventory honesty. If stock arrives in tiny batches and the alert goes out to far more people than there are units, the first wave buys and the rest are disappointed. Consider staggering alerts or prioritizing the earliest sign-ups, and say so in the message if that's the policy.

## The so-what

The first step is small. Check whether your sold-out product pages offer any way to be notified. If they just show a grayed-out button, add a notify-me option to your top five out-of-stock items this week, and set up one automated alert that fires when inventory returns.
