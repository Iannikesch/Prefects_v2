Agency pitches are built to sound impressive, and most business owners don't have the background to tell substance from polish. A handful of pointed questions will cut through most of it faster than any case study slide. Ask them early. It saves the much more expensive discovery that comes after the contract is signed.

## The eight questions

1. **Which specific channel drove your last ten closed customers, and how do you know?** This forces a specific answer. Hesitation is telling. An agency that tracks real outcomes can answer quickly.
2. **What attribution model do you use, and why that one?** If the answer is "whatever the platform defaults to," keep digging. A good answer explains how credit is assigned and what other methods, such as holdback tests, back it up.
3. **What happens when a campaign underperforms its target?** Listen for a specific process: who reviews, how fast, what gets changed. A general reassurance isn't a process.
4. **Can I see a monthly report from an existing client, with names redacted?** The report shows what the agency considers important. If it's all impressions and engagement, you know what to expect.
5. **How will my first-party data be used, and who owns it if we part ways?** This exposes agencies that build on their own accounts and databases and make leaving difficult. You want to own your ad accounts, your data and your history.
6. **What's an example of a campaign you'd call a failure, and what did you learn?** An agency with no failures is either lying or not testing. Look for a specific story and a change in approach that followed.
7. **Who specifically will work on my account day to day?** Not who's in the pitch meeting. Ask for names and roles, and ask to meet them before you sign.
8. **Can I talk to a client of similar size and industry?** Case studies are curated for the deck. A direct conversation surfaces details that never make it onto a slide. Call the reference, and ask what went wrong and how it was handled.

## Treat the vetting as a pilot

The vetting conversation is a small preview of working with the agency. Notice how quickly they respond, how specific their answers are, how they handle a question they can't answer, and whether they say "I'll find out" rather than bluff. Give the reference call the same attention as the pitch. Block real time for it.

## Score the answers

You don't need a formal rubric, but a simple 0 to 2 score per question helps you compare agencies fairly. Score 2 for a specific, evidence-backed answer, 1 for a reasonable but general answer, and 0 for a dodge. Anything under 10 out of 16 is a flag.

## These aren't traps

None of the questions are meant to be adversarial. A confident, well-run agency usually enjoys them, because it gives them the chance to show how they work. If an agency gets defensive about any of these, that reaction is useful information, too.

## Make it a habit

A thorough vetting process done once pays off beyond the first agency relationship. Businesses that ask these questions consistently develop a sharper sense of what good marketing looks like, and a better way to spot weak spots in their current setup.

## An example

Take an example. Two agencies are asked the same question: which channel drove your client's last ten customers? The first answers with a dashboard of impressions and a story about brand lift. The second pulls up a CRM export, shows six customers from search, three from email and one it can't attribute, and says so. The second answer is smaller and less polished, and it's more useful because it's checkable.

Run all eight questions the same way. Ask for specifics, ask for the person who would do the work and ask to see one real report with the client's name removed. Agencies that work from evidence answer quickly. Agencies that don't tend to answer with process language.

## What to do with the answers

Write the answers down side by side within an hour of each meeting. Memory favors the best presenter, and a short scorecard favors the best evidence.

Be consistent about who joins the conversation. If you ask one agency to bring the person who would run your account, ask all of them. The comparison is only fair when each agency is held to the same questions, the same format and the same follow-up, and when you write down what you were told.

## The so-what

Pick the three questions that matter most to you, send them in writing before the next pitch meeting, and ask each agency to answer in the room. Compare scores, call the references, then decide. If you already work with an agency, ask yourself the same questions about them.
