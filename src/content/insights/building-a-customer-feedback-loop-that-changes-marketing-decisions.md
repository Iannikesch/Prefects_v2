Most businesses collect customer feedback constantly, through support tickets, post-purchase surveys, and the occasional review, and very little of it ever reaches the person actually making marketing decisions. It sits in a support platform, a survey tool, or a review site, technically collected but functionally invisible to whoever's writing next month's email campaign or building next quarter's ad creative.

## Feedback rarely reaches marketing

The disconnect usually isn't a lack of feedback, it's a lack of a deliberate pathway connecting that feedback to the people who could actually act on it. A support team fielding the same product complaint repeatedly has valuable information about a messaging gap or an unmet expectation set by marketing copy, but that information rarely travels from a support ticketing system into a marketing team's actual planning process, since the two systems, and often the two teams, operate almost entirely independently of each other.

## An example

Consider a hypothetical case. A direct-to-consumer skincare brand's support team notices a recurring theme across incoming tickets: customers repeatedly express confusion about how quickly to expect visible results, leading to early returns from customers who assumed the product wasn't working after just a few days of use. That specific, recurring piece of feedback, surfaced through a simple monthly summary shared from support to marketing, directly informs a revision to the brand's product page copy and post-purchase email sequence, setting clearer expectations upfront. Return rate on that specific product drops measurably in the following quarter, a direct result of marketing content actually incorporating what support had been hearing for months without anyone previously connecting the two.

## Start with a monthly summary

Building this pathway doesn't require sophisticated new software in most cases, worth clarifying since the barrier here is usually organizational rather than technical. A simple, recurring practice, a monthly or biweekly summary of common themes from support tickets, reviews, and survey responses, shared directly with whoever owns marketing content and messaging, closes most of the gap without requiring an elaborate cross-departmental system. The discipline of actually doing this consistently matters more than the sophistication of whatever tool facilitates it.

Post-purchase surveys deserve a specific mention, since they're one of the more underused feedback sources relative to how easy they are to collect. A single, well-timed question, asked two to three weeks after a purchase once a customer has had genuine time to use a product, what almost stopped you from buying, or what question did you have before you purchased that wasn't answered, surfaces exactly the kind of objection or confusion a marketing team needs to address directly in its messaging, information that's otherwise difficult to gather any other way, since customers who did complete a purchase rarely think to volunteer what almost stopped them.

Review content, beyond its obvious use as social proof, functions as a rich, ongoing source of language customers actually use to describe a product or service, phrasing that often differs meaningfully from how a business's own internal team describes the same thing. Marketing copy built around a business's internal vocabulary sometimes misses the actual words and concerns real customers use when describing their own experience, and mining review language directly for phrasing and recurring themes tends to produce messaging that resonates more directly with how prospective customers are actually thinking about a purchase decision.

## Give someone ownership

Ownership of this process matters as much as the process itself. Feedback that belongs to everyone in theory tends to belong to no one in practice, and assigning a specific person, even someone splitting the responsibility alongside other duties, to actually compile and share these recurring themes on a set schedule is what turns a good intention into something that actually happens month after month.

Quantifying feedback themes, rather than relying purely on anecdotal impressions of what support or reviews seem to be saying lately, strengthens the case for actually acting on a specific theme. Tracking how many support tickets or reviews mention a specific recurring issue over a given month, rather than reacting to whichever complaint happens to be freshest in someone's memory, helps a marketing team prioritize the feedback that's widespread over the feedback that simply happened to be loudest or most recent.

Closing the loop back to customers who provided feedback, when a change actually gets made as a result, deserves a brief mention too, since it's an easy step that gets skipped constantly. A short follow-up acknowledging that a specific piece of feedback led to a real change, sent even to a small sample of the customers who originally raised the issue, reinforces that feedback gets used, which tends to encourage more thoughtful, detailed feedback from that same audience going forward rather than the brief, low-effort responses feedback requests often receive by default.

## The so-what

This month, ask support to send marketing a one-page summary of the top five recurring themes from tickets and reviews. Assign one owner and a recurring date. Then change one piece of copy based on what you find.
