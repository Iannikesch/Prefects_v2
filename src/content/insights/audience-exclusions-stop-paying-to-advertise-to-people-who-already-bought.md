Open the ad account and look at who's actually seeing the ads. Many businesses are surprised to find that a meaningful share of their impressions go to people who already bought, who are already in the pipeline, or who work at the company. Those impressions cost money and rarely produce a new customer.

## Who's seeing your ads?

An exclusion is simply an instruction to the ad platform: don't show this ad to these people. It sounds minor, and it's one of the cheapest efficiency gains in paid media, because it doesn't require better creative or smarter bidding. It just stops the waste.

The most common exclusion is existing customers. If a campaign exists to acquire new customers, showing it to people who already bought is a miss. Upload a list of customers (platforms support matching by email or phone, with hashing to protect the data) and exclude them from acquisition campaigns. Do the same with recent leads who are already talking to sales.

## Retargeting needs them too

Retargeting campaigns need exclusions too. Someone who visited the pricing page and then purchased shouldn't keep seeing 'Come back and buy!' for the next 30 days. Exclude purchasers from retargeting as soon as the purchase is recorded. It's embarrassing for the brand and expensive for the budget, and it happens constantly because nobody set up the exclusion.

## An example

Take an example with made-up numbers. A direct-to-consumer brand spending $15,000 a month on prospecting ads. An overlap analysis shows that about 22% of impressions are reaching existing customers. After uploading the customer list and excluding it, new-customer acquisition cost falls from $64 to $52, a drop of nearly 19%, with no change to creative or budget. The money that had been showing ads to people who'd already bought was redirected to people who hadn't.

## More exclusions to add

Exclude employees and internal users. Staff clicking on the company's own ads waste money and can distort performance data. Many platforms allow exclusion by IP address for website analytics, and some ad platforms let you exclude customer lists that include staff email addresses. It's a small thing that makes the data cleaner.

Use exclusions to keep offers appropriate. A new-customer discount shouldn't be shown to existing customers, who might feel cheated if they see a better deal than they received, or who might exploit it. Separate the campaigns by audience so each group sees the offer meant for it.

Exclude people who have already converted on the specific offer. A lead-generation campaign for a free consultation shouldn't continue to target people who booked one. A webinar promotion shouldn't chase registrants. The rule is the same: when a person completes the goal, remove them from the campaign aimed at that goal.

## Keep lists fresh and private

Keep the lists fresh. A customer list uploaded six months ago doesn't include anyone who bought since. Automate the update where possible, with a connection between the CRM or store and the ad platform, or schedule a monthly manual upload. A stale exclusion list quietly stops working.

Be aware that match rates aren't 100%. Platforms can only match the portion of a list that corresponds to their user accounts, so a list of 10,000 customers might match to 6,000 or 7,000 profiles. Exclusions will therefore be partial. That still captures most of the waste, but expect some existing customers to slip through.

Privacy matters here. Uploading customer data to an ad platform should align with the business's privacy policy and any consent requirements, and you should confirm the platform's terms for customer list use. Hashing helps with security but doesn't replace the need for a lawful basis to use the data. When in doubt, check with whoever handles privacy for the business.

Think beyond customers. Other groups often deserve exclusion: people who unsubscribed from emails, visitors to the careers page (who are job seekers rather than buyers), and users who already submitted a support request. A job seeker clicking a product ad costs money and produces nothing. A quick look at which pages signal non-buyer intent will suggest the right lists.

Exclusions can also keep campaigns from competing with each other. If two campaigns target overlapping audiences, the platform may bid one against the other, raising costs for both. Excluding the audience of one campaign from the other keeps them from fighting over the same people.

Be selective, though. Over-excluding can shrink an audience until delivery suffers, and some exclusions backfire. Excluding past customers from a campaign promoting a repeat-purchase product, for example, would remove your best prospects. Match each exclusion to the goal of the campaign, and review the audience size after applying it.

## The so-what

Spend ten minutes this week checking your largest prospecting campaign for an existing-customer exclusion. If there isn't one, upload your current customer list, apply it as an exclusion, and note the date. Compare cost per new customer over the following month against the month before.
