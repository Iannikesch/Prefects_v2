Reviews are the closest thing a local business has to a storefront window online. Prospects read them before they call, search engines weigh them in local results, and ad platforms surface them next to listings. Yet many businesses with happy customers have a dozen reviews, simply because nobody ever asked in a systematic way. A walk through a composite campaign shows what changes when someone does.

## Reviews are the storefront window

Here's a made-up scenario with invented numbers. A regional HVAC company with 12 Google reviews and a 4.4 rating after eight years in business. It completes about 600 jobs a year, so the supply of satisfied customers is large. The gap isn't service quality. It's the absence of a process: review requests happen when someone remembers, which is rarely.

## Step one: ask at the right moment

Step one is deciding when to ask. The best moment is right after a good experience, when the customer is pleased and the memory is fresh: the technician finishes the repair, the system works, the customer says thank you. The company builds the request into the job-completion process, so it triggers automatically when a job is marked complete.

## Step two: make it effortless

Step two is making it effortless. The request goes out by text within a few hours of the job, with a direct link to the review page. No searching, no logging in to six services, no instructions. Every extra step loses a percentage of people. A QR code on the invoice or the technician's tablet provides a second path for customers who prefer to do it on the spot.

## Step three: train the team

Step three is training the people who interact with customers. Technicians learn a simple, honest script: 'If you're happy with the work, a review would mean a lot to us. You'll get a text with a link.' It takes ten seconds, and it works better when it comes from the person the customer just dealt with. The company shares the ratings with the team, so staff see their names in the feedback.

## Step four: follow the rules

Step four is following the rules. Google and other platforms prohibit offering incentives in exchange for reviews, and they discourage 'review gating,' meaning only sending the review link to customers who've indicated they were satisfied. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews also restricts incentives tied to positive sentiment and the suppression of negative reviews. The company asks every customer, treats every response the same, and doesn't offer discounts or gifts for reviews. It's slower than cheating, but it keeps the profile safe.

## Step five: reply to every review

Step five is replying. Every review gets a response within a couple of days, positive or negative. Thanks, a specific detail from the job, and a signature from a real person. For negative reviews, the response is calm, factual, and offers to fix the problem offline. Prospects read the replies as carefully as the reviews, and a gracious response to criticism earns trust.

## What changes

Over twelve months, the numbers move. In this example, the company grows from 12 reviews to 140 and its rating settles at 4.8. The company asked roughly 600 customers and gained 128 reviews, a response rate of about 21%. Calls from its Google Business Profile rise by 38%, though part of that lift probably comes from other changes during the year, and the company wisely avoids claiming all of it.

Put the reviews to work beyond the listing. With each platform's rules in mind, the company can quote specific phrases on its website and in ads, such as customers mentioning same-day service or a clean job site. Real customer language tends to persuade better than the company's own descriptions, and it shows what customers value most.

## Keep it running

Track the process, not just the outcome. Count how many requests go out, how many customers click, how many reviews appear, and which technicians generate the most. If a technician's customers rarely respond, find out whether requests are being sent or whether something about the interaction needs a closer look.

Handle the occasional bad review as information. One unhappy customer isn't a disaster in a profile of 140 reviews, and the pattern in negative feedback may point at something fixable, such as scheduling delays or unclear estimates. A business that reads its criticism carefully gets a free operations audit. If a review is fake or violates the platform's policies, report it through the proper channel rather than retaliating.

Don't forget other platforms. Depending on the industry, customers may look at Yelp, Facebook, industry-specific sites, or Apple Maps. Concentrate effort on the platform that matters most for your category and market, and expand once the process is running. Spreading requests thinly across five sites from the start dilutes the result everywhere.

Make the ask part of the culture instead of a project. Mention reviews in team meetings, share the best ones, and thank staff by name when customers praise them. A request process that depends on one enthusiastic person fades when that person is busy or leaves, while one that's built into daily work keeps running.

## The so-what

Reviews compound. Each new one adds to the total, improves the rating's stability, and gives the next prospect more evidence. The first step is small. This week, add a review request text to your job-completion process and send it to the next ten customers. Count what comes back over the following month, then adjust the timing or the wording based on what you see.
