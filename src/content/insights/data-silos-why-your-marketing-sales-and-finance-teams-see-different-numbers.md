Ask three people in the same building for last month's revenue number and there's a decent chance you get three different answers, each one confidently sourced from whichever system that person happens to trust. Marketing pulls from the ad platform's own conversion tracking. Sales pulls from the CRM's closed-deal total. Finance pulls from the accounting system's actual recognized revenue. None of them are lying. They're just each looking at a different definition of the same word.

## Three systems, three definitions

Each department adopted a tool built for its own job. Each tool counts things differently by design.

An ad platform counts a conversion the moment a purchase event fires, sometimes before the refund window closes. A CRM counts a deal as closed when a rep updates a status field, sometimes before payment clears. An accounting system counts revenue only once cash or a recognized invoice hits the books, often weeks later.

That makes three legitimate definitions, three different totals, and nothing forcing them to agree.

## What it looks like in a meeting

Here's a hypothetical.A mid-size ecommerce brand holds its monthly leadership review, and marketing reports $340,000 in attributed revenue for the month based on ad platform conversion data. Finance reports $298,000 in actual recognized revenue for the same period once refunds, chargebacks, and payment processing delays are accounted for. Nobody in the room is wrong exactly, but the $42,000 gap becomes the entire conversation, eating twenty minutes that should have gone toward actually deciding what to do about performance, not reconciling whose number to trust.

## Label every number

Don't pick one department's number and force the others to adopt it. Each number serves a legitimate purpose for its own team.

Label instead. Tag every number in a shared report with its source and definition: platform-reported revenue, closed-deal revenue or recognized revenue. The $42,000 gap then stops being a mystery and becomes an expected difference between three systems measuring three things.

A single source of truth for the specific metrics that matter most across departments, even if it's just a shared dashboard pulling from all three systems side by side, does more to prevent this friction than any single meeting spent arguing about whose number is correct. That dashboard doesn't need to replace any department's own internal reporting. It just needs to exist as the place everyone agrees to look when a cross-department number gets discussed.

## Mind the timing lag

Timing lag compounds the confusion further, and it's worth naming separately from definitional differences. A sale that closes today in the CRM might not show up as recognized revenue in the accounting system for another two to four weeks, depending on payment terms and invoicing cycles. A team unaware of that lag can look at a month-over-month dip in recognized revenue and panic about a sales slowdown that hasn't actually happened, it's simply sitting in a different system's queue, waiting to be recognized on a slightly delayed schedule.

## Write the definitions down

There's an organizational habit worth building alongside any technical fix: a standing definition document, agreed on once by representatives from marketing, sales, and finance together, spelling out exactly what each department means by revenue, a lead, a customer, or any other term likely to get used across departments in a shared conversation. It takes one meeting to build and saves dozens of future meetings from relitigating the same confusion every time the numbers don't match at first glance.

None of this is unique to larger organizations either. Even a small business with a single owner juggling a CRM, an ad account, and a bookkeeping tool can run into the same definitional mismatch internally, simply talking to themselves across three different dashboards instead of three different departments. The fix scales down the same way it scales up: know which number means what before comparing it to anything else.

## Integrations help, up to a point

Software integration can reduce, though rarely eliminate, this friction over time. Connecting a CRM directly to an accounting platform, so closed deals automatically flow into a recognized-revenue pipeline with appropriate timing built in, removes some of the manual reconciliation that otherwise falls on whoever compiles a cross-department report each month. It doesn't erase the underlying definitional differences entirely, since a closed deal and recognized revenue are still different milestones, but it does reduce the amount of manual guesswork involved in explaining the gap when it shows up.

## The so-what

When someone cites a revenue or lead number in your next cross-department review, ask two things: which system did it come from, and what counts as included? If nobody can say, schedule one hour with marketing, sales and finance to agree on definitions for revenue, lead and customer, and write them down before the next quarterly number gets debated.
