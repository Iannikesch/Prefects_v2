Geofencing draws a virtual boundary around a specific place, such as a competitor's location, a neighborhood or an event venue, and serves ads to people who enter it. It sounds precise, and for the right use case it is. Whether it's worth the spend depends on the business and how the tactic is used.

## Where it works best

Where geofencing earns its reputation is in a fairly narrow set of use cases: a business targeting people physically near a specific competitor location, a business capitalizing on a known local event with a predictable audience, or a business with a hyper-local service area where standard radius targeting on a platform like Meta or Google is too broad to be useful. A home services company targeting a five-mile radius around a specific neighborhood undergoing new construction, for instance, is a strong fit for the tactic, since the audience and the geography are both tightly, meaningfully defined.

## Where it adds less

Where it tends to underdeliver is in broader, less specific applications, geofencing an entire city or a large shopping district with no particular reason for that boundary beyond general proximity to a business's storefront. In those cases, the precision that makes geofencing appealing in theory doesn't add much real targeting value beyond what a standard radius-based local ad campaign on a major platform already accomplishes, often at a lower cost per result and with better overall reach.

## An example

Here's a hypothetical with made-up numbers. A regional HVAC company geofences three specific competitor locations, serving ads only to devices detected within those boundaries over a two-week period. Cost per lead from that specific campaign comes in noticeably below the company's typical paid search cost per lead, and the leads themselves show a distinctly higher awareness of the company's specific competitive advantages, since the messaging was built directly around the competitive positioning relevant to that boundary. The same company, in the same quarter, also tries geofencing its entire metro area for a general brand-awareness push. That broader campaign produces a cost per lead roughly in line with a standard Meta local awareness campaign, but at a meaningfully higher total cost, since geofencing technology carries a premium over standard platform-native local targeting.

## Know the accuracy

Accuracy is a technical limitation worth understanding before committing real budget to the tactic. Geofencing relies on device location data, and depending on the specific technology and data provider behind a given platform, that accuracy can range from tight, within a matter of yards, to considerably looser, especially indoors or in dense urban areas where GPS signal gets less reliable. A business paying a premium for precise geofencing should ask a vendor directly what location accuracy their specific technology actually delivers, rather than assuming all geofencing products perform identically.

## Attribution is harder

Attribution complicates evaluating geofencing further, since a service triggered by physical location doesn't always convert immediately, or online at all. A homeowner who drives past a geofenced competitor location, sees an ad, and calls a business three days later from a completely different device may never show up as a tracked conversion tied back to that specific geofencing campaign, making the tactic's real return harder to measure cleanly than a standard digital ad click-to-conversion path.

## Ask about the fee

Cost structure deserves a direct mention too, since geofencing vendors often charge a premium over standard platform advertising for the technology itself, on top of media spend. A business evaluating the tactic should ask specifically what portion of the total bill is media spend reaching the actual audience versus a platform or technology fee layered on top, since that ratio varies significantly between vendors and directly affects whether the premium is actually worth paying for a given use case.

## Test small first

Testing a smaller, well-defined geofencing campaign before committing to a larger rollout is a reasonable way to evaluate the tactic without significant risk. A single competitor location or a single upcoming local event, run for a short, defined period with clear cost-per-lead tracking against the business's existing benchmarks, gives a real data point specific to that business rather than relying entirely on a vendor's general claims about what the technology can typically deliver.

## The so-what

Before buying geofencing, ask whether there's a specific, tightly defined location or event it fits. If yes, run one small test: a single competitor location or event for two weeks, tracked against your usual cost per lead. If the answer is a broad city-wide push, a standard local campaign will usually do the same job for less.
