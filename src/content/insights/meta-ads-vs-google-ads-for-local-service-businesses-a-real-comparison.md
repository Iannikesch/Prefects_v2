Plumbers, HVAC companies, movers and dentists get pitched Meta and Google ads constantly, sometimes by an agency that specializes in one platform. The two platforms solve different problems. Most local businesses need both, just not in equal proportion or at the same stage.

## Google captures demand that already exists

Someone searches "emergency plumber near me" because a pipe just burst. A well-run Google campaign puts you in front of them at the moment they're ready to call. Clicks can be expensive, but conversion rates tend to be strong because the person is already trying to buy and will decide quickly.

## Meta creates demand that wasn't there a minute ago

Someone scrolling Instagram wasn't thinking about their HVAC system until your seasonal maintenance offer appeared. Impressions are lower intent and generally cheaper. It suits promoting a specific offer or building awareness, not catching someone mid-emergency, because the person seeing the ad wasn't looking for a solution.

## Match the platform to how people buy

For urgent-need businesses like plumbing, HVAC repair or towing, Google should usually get the larger share, because that's where the customer in crisis is searching. Meta works as a supporting layer that keeps the brand visible, so it's familiar by the time someone does search. Familiar brands tend to see better click-through on Google, too.

For considered or discretionary services, such as remodeling, elective dental work or a planned move, Meta often deserves a bigger share. A homeowner might look at kitchen inspiration for weeks before typing a search. That browsing window is where Meta earns its keep.

## A starting split

| Business type | Google | Meta |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emergency repair (plumbing, towing) | 75% | 25% |
| Seasonal maintenance (HVAC tune-ups) | 60% | 40% |
| Planned projects (remodeling, moving) | 45% | 55% |

(A starting point to test from, not a rule.) The best number for you depends on how long your customers take to decide.

## Test both against booked jobs

Don't commit a full budget to either platform on faith. Run a time-boxed test on both at the same time and track cost per lead and, more importantly, cost per booked or closed job.

Say you spend $2,000 on each for 60 days. Google brings 40 leads at $50 each, and 14 book, a cost of $143 per job. Meta brings 80 leads at $25 each, and 8 book, a cost of $250 per job. Meta looks cheaper per lead and is 75% more expensive per job. For this business Google wins the booked-job race, though Meta may still be worth some budget if it warms people before they search. (Illustrative numbers.)

The comparison that matters isn't which platform gives cheaper clicks. It's which one produces customers at a cost you can sustain.

## Use them together

A hybrid approach many local businesses overlook: use Meta to retarget people who visited your website from a Google search but didn't convert. Google captures the high-intent visit, and Meta handles the follow-up nudge. That treats the two as a system instead of competing budget lines.

## Shift the split with the season

A fixed ratio all year is a missed opportunity. A moving company might lean on Google at peak moving season, when search intent spikes, and shift toward Meta in slow months to keep the brand visible ahead of the next rush. Review the split every quarter. A new competitor or a change in how people search can make a two-year-old ratio obsolete.

## An example

Take an example with made-up numbers. A drain and plumbing company spends $4,000 a month, split evenly between search ads and social ads. Search produces calls from people with burst pipes and clogged drains who need help today. Social produces fewer calls but reaches homeowners thinking about a water heater replacement months before it fails, and those leads convert at a lower rate but with larger tickets.

Judged on cost per lead alone, search wins easily. Judged on booked jobs and revenue, the picture is closer, and the social leads feed future search demand because more people already know the company's name. The takeaway isn't that one platform is better. It's that each does a different job, and the report should show the booked job, not only the lead.

A practical note on budget: if your total spend is small, start with one platform and add the second only once you can track booked jobs back to each. Splitting a very small budget across two platforms can leave both too thin to learn from, and the comparison will tell you little.

## The so-what

Look at your own sales data and find the lead time between someone realizing they need your service and buying. Short window: weight toward Google. Long window: you have room to build demand on Meta first. Then run the 60-day test above and let cost per booked job set your split.
