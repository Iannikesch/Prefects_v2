Every media buyer has watched an ad limp along for weeks past its useful life, propped up by a hunch that it might turn around. That instinct costs real money. The fix is a written rule for when an ad gets cut, decided before launch, when emotions and sunk cost haven't started clouding the call.

## Define "working" before the ad goes live

Pick the target in advance. Cost per lead under a set number. Return on ad spend above a threshold. Cost per booked job. Without it, every performance review turns subjective, and subjective reviews are how weak ads survive. It's always possible to argue that things might improve with a little more time.

Tie the target to sales where you can, not just clicks. A cheap lead that never closes can make a campaign look great while the business loses money.

## Give each ad a fair test

Don't judge too early. Killing an ad after twelve clicks is premature. Don't nurse one for weeks past its target either.

A workable minimum: enough spend to expect at least a few conversions at your target cost. If you want leads at $50 or less, an ad that has spent $150 with no leads has had a fair shot. If you're selling a $30 product with a $10 target cost per purchase, 100 clicks may be plenty. Match the sample to the number you need to see.

## Sample kill rules

| Situation | Rule |
| --- | --- |
| New ad, no conversions | Cut after spend reaches 3x target cost per result |
| Cost per result 50% above target | Cut after two straight weeks |
| Strong early, then decline | Refresh creative once, cut if no recovery in 7 days |
| Seasonal ad | Pause with a revisit date, don't delete |

(Illustrative thresholds. Set yours from your own margins.)

## Look for the pattern, not the bad day

One bad week can hit a good ad because of seasonality or a platform fluctuation. The signal to cut is cost per result climbing across several reporting periods, with no recovery after a creative swap. At that point the problem usually isn't the creative. It's the offer or the audience.

## Separate "the ad failed" from "the offer failed"

Sometimes the creative is fine and the price, offer or landing page is the problem. Killing the ad without fixing the offer means the next ad fails the same way and burns another round of test budget. Check the landing page and the offer before you blame the creative.

## Beat the sunk cost trap

The amount already spent on an ad has no bearing on whether to keep spending. A campaign that has burned $3,000 without converting deserves the same evaluation as a brand-new one. Ask: if I were launching this today, would I fund it?

## Pause or kill

Some ads are seasonal, and a rule that treats every dip as permanent can throw out creative that would perform again in the right month. Build in pause and revisit dates so reusable creative survives a temporary slump.

## Review weekly and keep a log

Check active ads on a set cadence, weekly for most accounts. A steady rhythm catches declines earlier, before they become expensive lessons. Waiting for a gut feeling means the problem has been running longer than it should.

When you cut an ad, write one line: what you cut, which metric triggered it and why you think it failed. Review the log quarterly. Over time it shows patterns specific to your audience and offers, which beat generic industry benchmarks.

## The so-what

Set the number today, before the next campaign launches, and write it where everyone can see it. A rule made in advance is much easier to follow than a judgment call made with money already spent. Then pull your three oldest active ads and ask the sunk-cost question: would you fund each one today?
