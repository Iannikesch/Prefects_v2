For a lot of service businesses, the most valuable conversion never happens on a website. A homeowner with a burst pipe doesn't fill out a form. They tap a phone number and call. If your ad reporting only counts form fills and online bookings, you may be judging campaigns on the smaller half of the picture.

## The blind spot

Phone calls are the blind spot. An ad platform can see a click. It can't see that the click turned into a six-minute call to your front desk, and it definitely can't see that the call became a $4,200 job. So the campaigns that drive calls look weaker than they are, and the ones that drive easy-to-track form fills look stronger than they deserve.

## Set up call tracking

The first fix is call tracking. Each marketing source gets its own phone number, or a pool of numbers is swapped dynamically onto the website so each visitor sees one tied to their session. When someone calls, the system records which ad, keyword, or page produced that call. Most call tracking tools also log duration, and many can record and transcribe the conversation. None of this is exotic. It's been around for years. It just doesn't get set up unless someone asks for it.

## Count only real calls

Duration matters more than people expect. A call under thirty seconds is usually a wrong number, a spam call, or someone who hung up. Treating every ring as a lead inflates results and trains ad platforms to chase cheap, useless calls. Set a minimum duration for what counts as a conversion, often somewhere between 60 and 120 seconds depending on the business, and send only the qualifying calls back to the ad platform.

## Close the loop on revenue

Then close the loop on revenue. A call is a lead, not a sale. The booked job, the accepted quote, the paid invoice all live in a scheduling tool or CRM. Matching the call record to the eventual job lets the business see which campaigns produce revenue instead of just ringing phones. Google Ads and Meta both accept offline conversion data, so the final outcome can flow back and shape how the campaigns optimize.

## An example

Consider a made-up case. A regional HVAC company running search ads and reviewing only web form conversions. By that measure the campaign looks mediocre: 14 leads in a month on $5,000 of spend, or about $357 each. After adding call tracking, the same campaign shows 63 qualifying calls on top of those forms. Cost per lead drops to about $65. Nothing about the ads changed. The measurement simply caught up with what customers were actually doing.

## Check call quality

Call quality is a second layer worth examining. Someone should listen to a sample of recorded calls (with proper disclosure, since recording laws vary by state, some require every party's consent, and when callers are in different states the stricter rule can apply, so many businesses announce recording at the start of every call). Are the callers asking for services the company actually offers? Is the front desk picking up? A campaign can drive plenty of calls that go unanswered, and that's a staffing problem showing up as a marketing problem.

## Store visits and answer rates

Store visits and walk-ins are harder, and there's no perfect answer. Some businesses ask 'how did you hear about us?' at the counter and log the reply, which is imperfect but better than nothing. Others use a unique offer code for each campaign. Platform-reported store visit numbers exist too, but they're modeled estimates, not counts, and they're best treated as directional.

Setup choices affect accuracy. A single tracking number printed on a truck or billboard can't tell you much, but a pool of dynamic numbers on the website can attribute individual sessions. The pool needs to be large enough for your traffic, or two visitors may see the same number and the attribution blurs. Ask the vendor how many numbers the plan includes and whether it's sized for your busiest weeks.

Then there's the old-fashioned problem of the number itself. Customers who find the business through a tracking number sometimes save it and call again months later, while the main number stays on the Google Business Profile and in local directories. Decide which number appears where, and keep the main listing consistent so directories don't end up showing conflicting phone numbers for the same company.

Missed calls deserve their own line in the report. A call that rang six times and rolled to voicemail is a lead that didn't convert for reasons that have nothing to do with the ad. Track answer rate alongside call volume. If 30% of tracked calls go unanswered during business hours, the cheapest improvement available isn't a new campaign. It's a person, a phone schedule, or an answering service.

Finally, share the call data with whoever answers the phones. When front desk staff hear that Tuesday's calls from the new campaign were mostly price shoppers, or that the water heater ad produces urgent callers who book on the spot, they can adjust how they answer. Marketing and operations stop blaming each other once they're reading the same numbers.

## The so-what

This week, count how many customers contacted you by phone after seeing an ad. If nobody can say, set up call tracking with a minimum duration and send the qualifying calls back to your ad platform. You can't improve a number you can't see.
