A weak brief produces weak work, and agencies sometimes get weak briefs. 'We want more leads.' 'We need a new website.' 'Help us grow on social.' These sentences describe a wish, not a problem, and an agency responding to them has to guess what success looks like. Sometimes it guesses wrong, and the business pays for the lesson.

## Keep it short and specific

A good brief isn't long. One or two pages is plenty. What matters is that it answers the questions an agency needs answered before it can propose anything sensible. Starting with a clear picture of the business, the goal, and the constraints saves weeks of back-and-forth and gets better proposals from every agency that sees it.

## Context, goal and baseline

Begin with context. What does the business sell, to whom, at what price, and how do customers currently find it? What's the sales process, and how long does it take? A paragraph on this prevents the agency from proposing things that make no sense for the way you actually sell, such as an ecommerce strategy for a business whose sales happen by phone.

State the goal in numbers. 'Increase qualified leads from 40 to 70 a month within six months at a cost per lead no higher than $90' is a goal. 'Grow awareness' isn't. If you don't know the right number, say so and ask the agency to help set one, but come with a starting point and a way you'll judge the outcome.

Include the baseline. Share current traffic, leads, conversion rates, spend, and revenue by channel, to the extent you have them. An agency that sees real numbers can spot opportunities and flag problems immediately. One that has to guess is likely to propose generic activity. If tracking is poor, say that too, since fixing it may be the first job.

## What's been tried and the constraints

Describe what's been tried. Which channels, which campaigns, what worked, what flopped, and why you think so. This prevents the agency from proposing something you tested last year and spares you the experience of paying someone to rediscover it. Include previous agencies or freelancers, within reason. The history explains a lot.

Share your constraints. Budget range, timeline, staffing, brand rules, legal or regulatory limits, systems that can't be changed, and any decisions already made. An agency that knows you have $4,000 a month and a team of two can design something that fits. One that learns this after a presentation wastes everyone's time.

Name the decision-makers and the process. Who approves work? How long does approval usually take? Who will be the day-to-day contact? Many agency relationships stall because the person who briefed the agency isn't the one who decides, and the real decider hasn't been in the room. Clarify before the work starts.

## An example

Take an example with made-up numbers. Picture two briefs for the same dental practice. The first reads: 'We want more patients. Please send ideas.' The second states that the practice serves a 15-mile radius, new patients are worth about $1,800 over three years, it currently gets 22 new patients a month and wants 35, the front desk can handle 20 more calls a week, it has $5,000 a month to spend, and its last agency focused on social posts without tracking calls. The second brief produces proposals that can be compared. The first produces a stack of sales decks.

## Examples, format and review

Share examples. Point to brands, ads, or websites you admire and ones you dislike, and say why. A single example of a tone you want to avoid can save an agency three rounds of drafts. It also reveals how you think about your own brand, which is information an agency can't easily get any other way.

Ask for proposals in a consistent format. Request that each agency state its understanding of the problem, the proposed approach, the people involved, the expected timeline, the measurement plan, and the pricing. Comparing six agencies is easier when they're answering the same questions, and the differences in thinking become obvious.

Treat the brief as the start of a conversation. A good agency will ask questions, challenge assumptions, and sometimes push back on the goal itself. That's a sign of engagement, not of difficulty. The agencies that accept every instruction without a question may not be thinking about your business at all.

Agree on how the work will be reviewed. Say how often you'll meet, what you'll look at, and how you'll decide if the plan is working. Agencies appreciate knowing the scorecard before they start, and it keeps early enthusiasm from drifting into vague satisfaction. A line or two in the brief about reporting expectations saves an awkward conversation three months in.

Update the brief as things change. A brief written in January may not describe the business in June, and an agency working from stale instructions can do excellent work on a problem that's no longer the problem. Revisit it quarterly, and note what's different.

## The so-what

Before you contact an agency, write a one-page brief that covers context, a numeric goal, the baseline, what's been tried, constraints, and who decides. Send it with your first message. You'll get better proposals, and the process of writing it will probably clarify your own thinking more than you expect.
