Eventually every marketing team asks the same question: should we move money from one channel to another? The usual answer comes from a hunch or from whichever channel report looks best that week. A structured reallocation takes more discipline, and it's much less likely to produce a quarter you regret.

## Start with marginal cost

Here's a hypothetical with made-up numbers. A company spending $20,000 a month: $9,000 on paid social, $7,000 on search, and $4,000 on display. It runs at an average cost per new customer of $110 across the account. Leadership wants to know whether to shift money, and where. The first step is to resist the temptation to rank channels by average cost per customer and move everything to the winner.

Average cost and marginal cost are different things. Search might average $95 per customer across its $7,000, but the last $1,000 added to search may buy customers at $130, because the cheapest, highest-intent searches were captured first. Display might average $210, yet its returns might be flat across the range. The decision depends on what the next dollar does, not what the average dollar did.

Estimating marginal cost doesn't require advanced modeling. Look at times the budget changed. If search spend rose from $5,000 to $7,000 last spring, compare the extra customers it produced to the extra money spent. That's a rough marginal cost. It isn't precise, but it's far better than assuming the average holds.

In this example, the analysis suggests search's marginal cost at the current level is about $130 per customer, while display's average, and probably marginal, cost is about $210. Moving $2,000 from display to search loses roughly 9.5 display customers and gains about 15.4 search customers, a net gain of about six customers a month on the same total spend.

## Move in small steps

Move in small steps. Shifting a modest slice, say 10% to 15% of the budget at a time, limits the damage if the analysis is wrong and lets the team watch what happens. Dramatic reallocations make results impossible to interpret, because too much changes at once, and can trigger learning resets in automated campaigns.

## Set the test rules

Set the test period and success measure in advance. Decide, for example, that the shift runs for eight weeks and will be judged on total new customers and blended cost per customer across the whole account, not on the channel's own dashboard. Channel-reported numbers can shift when another channel's spend changes, because of overlapping credit.

Watch for spillover. Cutting display might reduce brand searches a few weeks later, if display was quietly feeding search demand. This is exactly the kind of effect that single-channel reporting misses. Track branded search volume, direct traffic, and total conversions during the test, so that a hidden dependency shows up before it's too late.

Account for lag. Some channels deliver customers quickly, and others influence them over weeks. Judging a reallocation after seven days will favor the fast channels. Judge it over a period long enough to cover the typical path from first touch to purchase, which for many businesses is a month or more.

Set floors and ceilings. A channel that's cut to near zero can't be evaluated fairly and may be hard to restart, since audiences and learning are lost. A minimum level that keeps each channel alive, and a maximum beyond which the account hasn't been tested, protects against swings that look decisive and prove costly.

## Keep a decision log

Keep a decision log. Write down what was moved, why, what the expectation was, and when the review will happen. When the result comes in, compare it to the expectation. Over a year, the log builds into a record of how well the team's judgment holds up, and it prevents the same debate from restarting every quarter.

Don't forget capacity and seasonality. If search demand is seasonal, a shift that works in spring may fail in winter. If the sales team can't absorb more leads, extra customers from search won't convert. Check the surrounding conditions before concluding that a result will persist.

Check your data quality. Cost-per-customer figures from different platforms rely on different attribution rules, and the same customer may be claimed by two of them. Before moving money, reconcile the platforms' reported customers against the CRM or order system, and use the business's own count as the source of truth. A reallocation based on inflated platform numbers just moves the inflation around.

Tell the people affected. When spend moves from one channel to another, whoever manages the reduced channel may see their numbers drop and wonder why. Explain the reasoning and the test plan beforehand, and share the results afterward, whichever way they go. A team that understands why money moved is more likely to help the experiment work than to defend its own budget.

Don't confuse a reallocation with an increase. If the total budget changes at the same time as the mix, the effects blur. Where possible, hold the total steady during the test, and treat any budget increase as a separate decision afterward.

## The so-what

Before your next reallocation, write down what you expect to happen, how long the test will run and how you'll judge it on total account results. Move 10% to 15% of the budget, hold the total steady, and check against your own CRM numbers afterward.
