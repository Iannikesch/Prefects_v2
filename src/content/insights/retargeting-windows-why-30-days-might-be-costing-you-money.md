A retargeting window, the length of time someone stays in a retargeting audience after visiting a site, defaults to 30 days on most ad platforms, and most businesses never touch that setting again after the initial campaign gets built. That default wasn't chosen based on any particular business's actual sales cycle. It's just the number the platform ships with, and treating it as correct without checking is one of the quieter ways retargeting budget gets wasted.

## Match the window to the sales cycle

The right window depends entirely on how long it typically takes a customer to move from interest to purchase, and that varies enormously by product and price point. A business selling a $30 impulse item has little reason to keep retargeting someone 25 days after their last visit, since the purchase decision, if it was going to happen, almost always happens within the first handful of days. Continuing to spend impressions on that visitor a month later is largely wasted budget chasing someone whose window of genuine interest closed weeks earlier.

A business selling something with a longer, more considered purchase cycle, a piece of furniture, a service contract, anything in the multiple-hundreds-or-thousands range, often needs a longer window, sometimes 60 or 90 days, to catch customers who are still working through a slower decision process. A 30-day default cuts off retargeting right around when a lot of these customers are actually getting close to deciding, missing exactly the audience the campaign should be trying hardest to reach.

## Two mistakes in one account

Here's an illustration of both mistakes at once, using made-up numbers.A specialty food brand selling a $25 subscription box runs retargeting on the platform default 30-day window and finds that most conversions from retargeted visitors happen within the first four days, meaning roughly 26 days of that window are spent showing ads to people who were never going to convert from that visit anyway. Meanwhile, a kitchen remodeling company in the same ad account runs the same 30-day default and finds a meaningful share of its retargeted conversions happening between day 35 and day 60, well outside a window the platform had already closed, meaning that business was cutting off retargeting right as some of its best prospects were finally ready to convert.

## Tier the windows

Tiered retargeting windows solve both problems at once for businesses running multiple products or services with different sales cycles. Rather than one blanket window applied to every visitor, separate audiences with separate window lengths, matched to how long each specific product or service actually takes to convert, keep spend concentrated where it's actually working rather than spread evenly regardless of fit.

## Frequency and exclusions

Frequency capping matters alongside window length, and the two get confused often. A window that's the right length but shows the same ad to the same person 40 times over that period isn't more effective than showing it 8 times, it's usually less effective, since repetition past a certain point produces fatigue and, in some cases, active annoyance rather than increased purchase intent. Getting the window right and getting the frequency right are two separate settings that both need attention, not one fix that solves the other automatically.

Also exclude people who already bought. Showing "come back and buy" to a customer who purchased yesterday wastes impressions and can annoy them. Where it fits, build the next message from what each person actually viewed, so a visitor who looked at one product sees a follow-up for that product.

## Use your own data

Finding the right window for a specific business doesn't require guesswork. Most CRMs or analytics platforms can pull a simple report showing the actual gap between first site visit and eventual purchase across recent customers, and that distribution, not a platform default built for no one in particular, should be what determines where a retargeting window actually gets set.

## Check the season

Seasonality can shift the ideal window too, in ways worth checking rather than assuming stay constant year-round. A retailer selling holiday gifts might see a naturally shorter consideration window in the weeks right before a major shopping date, as urgency compresses the decision timeline, compared to the same product browsed in an off-season month with no deadline pressure attached. A single fixed window applied year-round can end up slightly wrong in both directions depending on the time of year, running too long during a high-urgency season and possibly too short during a slower one.

## The so-what

Pull the time between first visit and purchase for your last 100 customers. Set the window to cover roughly 80% of them, add a frequency cap, and exclude buyers. A single afternoon of work fixes a setting that runs every day your campaigns are live.
