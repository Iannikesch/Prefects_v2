Every few months someone publishes a study declaring the best time to send an email, usually Tuesday at 10 a.m. or something close. Marketers copy the advice, send at the recommended hour, and feel responsible. The trouble is that the study measured somebody else's audience.

## Whose audience was studied?

Send time does matter, but less than the headlines suggest and differently for different lists. A list of office workers behaves differently from a list of restaurant owners, who are slammed at noon and free at 3 p.m. A list of parents behaves differently again. The 'best' time is a property of the audience, not a universal truth.

Here's a wrinkle that undermines a lot of older advice: open rates are unreliable now. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, introduced in 2021, preloads emails for many Apple Mail users, which can register an open whether or not the person ever looked. A send-time test judged on opens may be partly measuring a robot. Use clicks, or better, conversions, as the judge.

## Test it yourself

Testing it yourself is simple. Split the list into comparable segments, send the same email at two different times, and compare click and revenue results. One test tells you little, because small samples are noisy. Run the comparison several times across a couple of months before concluding anything. If the difference stays small, the practical answer is that timing isn't where your gains are.

## An example

Consider a hypothetical case. A regional home goods retailer that tests Tuesday morning against Thursday evening across six sends. In this hypothetical, Thursday evening wins on revenue per recipient in four of the six, by an average of 11%. That's worth adopting. But the same retailer also tests subject lines in the same period and finds a 23% swing from a single change in the offer. The lesson isn't that timing is worthless. It's that timing is usually a smaller lever than the content of the message.

## Personalized send times

Some platforms offer send-time optimization that picks a personalized time for each recipient based on past behavior. It can help, mostly for large lists with plenty of history per subscriber. For a small list, there may not be enough data per person for the prediction to mean anything. Ask what the feature is actually learning from before trusting it.

Context beats clock time for certain messages. A cart reminder works best soon after abandonment, whatever the hour. A weekend flash sale should land before the weekend, not after. Event-driven messages follow the event. Scheduling for those should be built around the customer's action instead of a fixed slot on a calendar.

## Habits, audience and time zones

Frequency and consistency interact with timing. Sending at roughly the same time each week lets subscribers form a habit and lets inbox providers see a predictable sender. Jumping between 6 a.m. Monday and 9 p.m. Saturday for no reason adds variables to every comparison you try to make later, which makes the whole exercise harder to read.

Audience differences are worth exploring. Business-to-business lists often respond to messages sent during working hours, since people check email at their desks. Consumer lists, especially for retail, may respond better in the evening or on weekends when people have time to browse. Time zones complicate any single send time too. A national list sent at 10 a.m. Eastern lands at 7 a.m. for readers on the West Coast.

Day of the week deserves the same skepticism as hour. A restaurant promoting a Friday special wants the message earlier in the week or on Friday morning, not on Saturday. A tax preparer's messages follow a seasonal rhythm that dwarfs any weekly pattern. Look for the pattern that follows your customers' own routines, and let that guide the test calendar.

Inbox competition is a real factor, though harder to measure. Certain days, around major shopping events for example, see inboxes crammed with promotions, and a message landing among 200 others is easier to miss. Sending slightly off-peak sometimes helps and sometimes doesn't. That's exactly why testing beats copying someone else's advice.

## Keep the test clean

Keep the test clean. Change only the time, and keep the subject line, content, and audience selection identical. Randomize which subscribers receive which version. If one group is your most loyal customers and the other is your least engaged, the result tells you about the groups, not the hour.

Record results somewhere that survives staff changes. A simple log of send time, segment, clicks, and revenue per recipient builds into a real answer over a few months, and it saves the next person from rerunning the same experiments. It also gives you something concrete to show when a stakeholder asks why the schedule looks the way it does.

## The so-what

Pull your last ten campaigns and sort them by send time against clicks or revenue per recipient. If a clear pattern shows up, test it deliberately over the next two months. If nothing does, stop agonizing about the hour and spend the effort on the offer instead.
