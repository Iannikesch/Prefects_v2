In most businesses, a small group of customers accounts for a disproportionate share of revenue. The exact split varies, and you should look at your own numbers rather than trust a rule of thumb, but the pattern is common enough that it's worth checking. Yet the typical marketing program treats everyone the same, sending the best customers the same discounts as a first-time browser.

## A few customers, most of the revenue

Finding your VIPs is simple. Pull the last 12 months of orders, add up revenue per customer, rank them, and look at the top tenth. Then look at what they have in common: how often they buy, what they buy, how they found you. This exercise often produces surprises. The most valuable group may not be the one anyone assumed.

Here's a made-up scenario with invented numbers. An apparel brand with 12,000 customers and $2.4 million in annual revenue. The top 10%, 1,200 people, generated about $1.1 million of it, or roughly 46%. Those customers placed an average of 6.3 orders a year, compared with 1.4 for everyone else. Yet in the brand's email program, both groups received the same weekly promotion with the same 20% code.

That sameness has a cost. A VIP who'd have bought at full price receives a discount and keeps the difference as a gift. A VIP who gets the same generic email as a one-time buyer feels, reasonably, unrecognized. And the brand loses the chance to treat its best customers in a way that deepens a relationship worth far more than the average one.

## Better treatment, not bigger discounts

Better treatment doesn't have to mean bigger discounts. Early access to new products. A direct line to a person who knows them. Free shipping with no minimum. A handwritten note with an order. Invitations to a preview event. These cost little and signal that the business notices. For many loyal customers, being recognized matters more than saving ten percent.

Define VIP carefully. Spend alone can mislead. A customer who bought one $3,000 item and returned half of it isn't the same as one who has bought steadily for four years. Consider frequency, margin, recency, and returns, not just total spend, and revisit the definition once a year. A simple scoring approach, even in a spreadsheet, works fine.

Exclude VIPs from blanket promotions, at least some of them. If the top segment is likely to purchase anyway, sending them the sitewide sale code erodes margin on the people least in need of persuasion. Instead, send them something different: early access instead of a discount, or a gift with purchase rather than a price cut.

Watch for early warning signs. A VIP who normally orders every five weeks and hasn't ordered in ten is worth a personal check-in, not an automated 'we miss you' blast. Build a simple alert for VIPs whose purchase frequency drops, and route it to a human. Losing one of your best customers quietly costs far more than losing an average one.

## Ask and measure

Ask them for things only they can give. A short survey about what they love and what's missing. A request for a review or a referral, which carry weight coming from a proven customer. A seat on a small feedback panel. Loyal customers often enjoy being asked, and their answers are better informed than those of casual buyers.

Be mindful of fairness and transparency. If a VIP program is visible, as in tiers with named benefits, customers who miss the cut may feel excluded, and that needs handling gracefully. If it's invisible, as in quiet extras and early access, there's less friction. Either approach can work. Choose the one that suits the brand's relationship with its customers.

Measure the effect. Compare VIP retention, order frequency, and revenue before and after the new treatment, and where possible, against a comparison group that didn't receive it. VIP programs can cost real money in perks and staff time, and they should be held to the same standard of evidence as any other investment.

Service matters as much as marketing for this group. A VIP who's put on hold, receives a late shipment, or can't reach anyone with a problem will feel the gap more sharply than a casual buyer would. Flag VIPs in the support system so that issues get routed to someone empowered to fix them quickly. Often the best VIP perk is simply not having to wait.

Start small. Pick one change for the next quarter, such as removing VIPs from the blanket discount or adding an early-access email for them, and measure it. A modest program run consistently beats an elaborate one launched and forgotten, and each result shows what this particular group values.

## The so-what

Pull your last 12 months of orders this week and rank customers by profit. Then pick one change for your top 10%, such as removing them from the blanket discount or adding early access, and measure it next quarter.
