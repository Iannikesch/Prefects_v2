'Full-service' is one of the most common phrases on an agency website: strategy, creative, paid media, email, SEO, social, web development and analytics, all under one roof. The pitch is convenience, with one team, one invoice and coordinated work. It's an appealing model, and it works well when the depth behind each service is real. Here's how to check.

## Why coordination helps

Full-service does offer real advantages in some situations. A small business with limited management time may benefit from a single point of contact who coordinates channels. Coordinated messaging across email, ads, and the website can produce better results than disconnected efforts. The point isn't that integration is worthless. It's that integration shouldn't be mistaken for competence in each part.

## Where depth varies

The structural problem is that excellence in any one discipline takes sustained focus. Paid search specialists spend years learning the quirks of bidding, match types, and platform changes. Email specialists live in deliverability and automation. A team that covers ten disciplines can't have its best people in all ten, so the question is where the real strength sits and where the agency is coasting.

Many agencies are stronger in some disciplines than in others. That's normal. What matters is whether you know where the strengths are and whether the services you plan to buy are the ones the team does best.

## Ask who does each piece

Ask directly who handles each service you're considering, whether it's in-house or with a partner, and who the day-to-day person is. A good agency answers this plainly. Knowing the structure lets you judge the work for what it is.

## An example

Here's a hypothetical. A restaurant group choosing between a full-service agency and a specialist firm that only does local search and paid social. The full-service agency's proposal includes eleven services at one monthly fee. The specialist's proposal covers two. The full-service pitch looks like better value. But when the group asks for results in the two channels that actually drive its reservations, the specialist produces detailed numbers from comparable restaurants and the generalist offers broad case studies built mostly around other services.

## Questions that reveal depth

A useful question to ask is what percentage of the agency's clients buy each service, and what percentage of its revenue it represents. If paid media is 60% of revenue and SEO is 5%, then the agency's SEO capability is probably thin, however prominently it appears on the services page. The answer shows where the agency's real expertise lies.

Ask for specifics by channel. For every service you intend to buy, request examples with numbers from clients in similar situations, the name of the person who would run it, and how many accounts that person manages. A specialist at a full-service agency should be able to answer easily. Vague answers about 'our integrated approach' suggest the specialty isn't really there.

## Scope what you need

Start by deciding what you actually need. If 80% of your leads come from two channels, you probably don't need eleven services, however convenient the package looks. Paying for capabilities you won't use subsidizes the agency's breadth, and it can distract the team from the channels that matter. Buy the services your revenue depends on, and add others only when the case is clear.

The alternative isn't necessarily a roster of separate specialists, which can create its own coordination burden. Some businesses do well by choosing an agency that's excellent at the channel driving most of its revenue, then adding specialists for secondary needs, with a clear owner on the business side to keep it all pointed in one direction.

## Check the price and the contract

Price can be a signal. A full-service retainer that seems low for the number of services offered often means each service gets a small slice of someone's time. Calculate roughly how many hours per month each service could receive at the price quoted. If the number is a handful of hours for something that normally needs dozens, the breadth is cosmetic.

Contracts should describe deliverables in specific terms, not just service names. 'SEO' could mean a monthly report or a serious program of content, technical fixes, and link work. 'Social media management' could mean three posts a week or a full community strategy. Pin the scope down so that you and the agency share the same picture of what's being bought.

Ask to speak with a current client who buys only some of the services. A client who uses the agency for paid media and nothing else can describe how the work really runs, without the polish of the full package. Their account of responsiveness and depth is usually more telling than the agency's own account of its integrated approach.

## The so-what

Before you sign with any agency, full-service or not, list the services you actually plan to use, then ask for numbers, a named lead and a current-client reference for each one. Pin the deliverables down in the contract. Depth you can verify beats breadth you can't.
