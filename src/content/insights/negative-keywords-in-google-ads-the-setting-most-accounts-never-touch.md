A Google Ads account bidding on the keyword plumber will, without a negative keyword list actively maintained, also show up for searches like plumber salary, plumber jokes, and how to become a plumber, none of which represent anyone remotely close to hiring a plumbing service. Every one of those irrelevant clicks costs real money and produces zero value, and it's one of the most common, most fixable sources of wasted paid search spend that most small and mid-market accounts simply never address.

## Why it matters

Negative keywords work as a filter, telling Google's ad system which search terms should never trigger a specific ad, even if the search technically contains a keyword the campaign is bidding on. Without an actively maintained negative keyword list, a campaign relies entirely on Google's own broad matching logic to decide relevance, and that logic, while improving over time, still regularly serves ads against searches with only a loose, tangential connection to what a business actually offers.

## An example

Take an example with made-up numbers. A residential plumbing company runs a Google Ads campaign bidding broadly on plumbing-related terms with no negative keyword list in place. A search terms report, pulled after several weeks of spend, reveals that roughly 18% of total ad spend went toward clicks on searches like plumbing apprenticeship, plumber salary Texas, and plumbing tools for sale, none of which represent a potential customer looking to hire a plumber. Adding a negative keyword list built specifically around these irrelevant patterns, terms like salary, jobs, apprenticeship, and tools, redirects that wasted spend toward relevant searches instead, producing a meaningfully lower cost per qualified lead across the same total budget.

## Keep the list current

Building an effective negative keyword list isn't a one-time setup, worth stressing directly, since new irrelevant search patterns emerge continuously as language and search behavior shift. A search terms report, reviewed regularly, ideally weekly for a newer or higher-spend campaign, monthly at minimum for anything more established, surfaces new irrelevant queries as they appear, letting a business add fresh negative keywords before those patterns accumulate into meaningful wasted spend over an extended stretch of time.

## Account vs campaign level

There's a layered approach worth understanding for accounts running multiple campaigns simultaneously. Negative keywords can be applied at the account level, blocking a term across every campaign at once, or at the individual campaign level, blocking a term only within a specific campaign while still allowing it elsewhere. A business running both a residential and a commercial plumbing campaign, for instance, might want to exclude commercial from the residential campaign specifically, while still allowing that same term to trigger ads within the commercial campaign, a distinction only campaign-level negative keywords can achieve.

## Pick match types on purpose

Match type matters here too, and it's a detail worth getting right rather than defaulting to whatever setting happens to be pre-selected. A negative keyword set to broad match will block a wider range of related searches, sometimes too wide, occasionally excluding a legitimately relevant search along with the irrelevant ones it was meant to catch. A negative keyword set to exact match blocks only that precise search term, offering more precision but requiring a longer, more actively maintained list to catch every variation of an irrelevant pattern. Most accounts benefit from a mix of both, broad negatives for clearly irrelevant categories and exact negatives for specific, narrower exclusions.

## Shared lists

There's a shared-list feature worth knowing about for agencies or businesses managing several accounts or campaigns at once. Rather than rebuilding a similar negative keyword list from scratch in every new campaign, a shared negative keyword list can be built once and applied across multiple campaigns simultaneously, saving considerable setup time and ensuring a consistent baseline of common exclusions, salary, jobs, free, DIY, gets applied everywhere it's relevant without requiring separate manual entry each time.

## Don't overdo it

There's a caution worth naming on the other side of this practice too, since being too aggressive with negative keywords carries its own cost. Excluding a term that seemed obviously irrelevant but actually captured a meaningful share of genuine customer intent can quietly cut off real business without anyone noticing right away, since a missed opportunity rarely shows up as clearly in a report as wasted spend does. Reviewing the impact of a new negative keyword addition after a few weeks, checking whether overall lead volume dropped unexpectedly, helps catch an overly broad exclusion before it costs more than the waste it was meant to prevent.

## The so-what

Pull your search terms report this week if it's been more than a month. Add the clearly irrelevant patterns as negatives, then recheck lead volume in a few weeks to confirm you didn't block real demand. It's usually under an hour of work.
