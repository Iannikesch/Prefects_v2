Anyone can run an A/B test. Doing it so the result means something is harder. A clean test has a hypothesis, one change, a fair split, enough traffic, and a decision rule made before the results come in. Skip any of those, and the outcome is a chart that feels scientific and isn't.

## Write a hypothesis

Start with the hypothesis, in a sentence. 'Changing the headline from a feature statement to a customer outcome will increase form submissions.' It names the change and the expected effect. Without a hypothesis, tests turn into random redesigns, and a win teaches nothing that can be applied to the next page.

## Change one thing

Change one meaningful thing. If the new version has a different headline, a different image, a new form, and a new button color, a win can't be traced to any of them. Pick the element most likely to matter, usually the headline or the offer, since these tend to produce larger differences than button colors or font sizes.

## Set sample size and duration first

Decide the sample size and duration in advance. A rough rule is to wait until each version has at least a few hundred visitors and, ideally, 50 or more conversions, though exact requirements depend on how big a difference you want to detect. Free sample-size calculators exist. And run the test for full weeks, since behavior differs between Tuesday and Saturday, and stopping on a Wednesday can skew the result.

## An example

Take an example with made-up numbers. A software company testing its trial sign-up page. Version A keeps the headline 'Powerful Invoicing for Small Business.' Version B says 'Get Paid Faster: Send Your First Invoice in Five Minutes.' Traffic is split 50/50 for 28 days. A receives 2,310 visitors and 92 sign-ups (4.0%). B receives 2,290 visitors and 118 sign-ups (5.2%).

Is B really better? That's where a significance check comes in. With these numbers, the difference is large enough that it's unlikely to be luck, which a calculator confirms. Had the counts been 92 and 99, the right conclusion would be 'no clear winner,' and the honest move would be to test something bolder.

## Check segments and downstream results

Check the segments before declaring victory. Did B win on mobile and desktop? On paid traffic and organic? A headline that works for one audience can fail for another, and an overall win may hide a loss in your most valuable segment. Don't slice the data a dozen ways hoping something shows up, since more slices mean more chances of a false finding. Look at the two or three splits that matter most.

Then look downstream. More sign-ups only help if they become customers. Follow the trial users from each version for a few weeks. If B's sign-ups activate and convert at the same rate as A's, great. If B's drop off faster, the headline may be promising something the product doesn't deliver, and the apparent win is a mirage.

## Don't peek

Resist peeking. Checking results daily and stopping the moment one version pulls ahead is the most common way tests go wrong. Early leads often shrink or reverse as more data arrives. Set the end date, and look at the final numbers once. If you must monitor, watch for technical problems, such as a broken form on one version, not for winners.

## Log everything

Afterward, write the result down, including the tests that found nothing. A log of what was tried and what happened keeps a team from repeating failed ideas and builds a record of what this particular audience responds to. Over a year, that record is worth more than any single win.

Traffic quality can sink a test without anyone noticing. If one version happens to receive more visitors from a particular ad campaign because of how the split was configured, the result reflects the campaign, not the page. Check that the two groups look similar in source, device, and geography before trusting the outcome. Most testing tools show these breakdowns, and a mismatch is a reason to rerun.

## When traffic is low

Low traffic is a common obstacle for small businesses. A page with 600 visitors a month can't detect a 10% improvement in any reasonable time. Options include testing bolder changes, which produce bigger differences and need less data, combining traffic from several pages with similar goals, or running the test longer and accepting a slower learning cycle. What not to do is declare a winner from a few dozen conversions.

## Carry the lesson forward

When a variant wins, ship it, then ask what the result suggests about the audience. If a benefit-led headline beat a feature-led one, that insight may apply to ads, emails, and other pages too. The most valuable output of a test is rarely the single page improvement. It's the lesson about what this audience cares about, carried into the next piece of copy.

## The so-what

Write your next landing page test as a single sentence with a hypothesis, one change, a sample size, and a stop date, before you touch the page. Put it somewhere your team can see it. Then hold yourself to it, including when the early numbers tempt you to call it early.
