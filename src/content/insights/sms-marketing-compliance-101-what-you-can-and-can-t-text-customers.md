SMS works because it's direct. That directness also comes with legal weight that email never carried in quite the same way. The Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA) governs commercial texting in the United States. Statutory damages can reach $500 per message, and up to $1,500 per message if a court finds the violation willful. Class actions over unwanted texts are common, and some law firms specialize in them.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules change, and your counsel should review your program.

## The core rule is consent

You need prior express written consent before sending marketing texts. It has to be specific to texting. It can't be bundled quietly into some other agreement, and it can't be implied.

A customer giving you a phone number at checkout is not consent to receive promotions. That gap trips up more businesses than almost anything else, usually because the checkout flow was built for speed rather than compliance.

Consent also doesn't carry over between channels. An email subscriber of five years hasn't agreed to receive texts. Each channel needs its own documented opt-in.

## What recent rule changes mean

In early 2025 a federal appeals court vacated the FCC's "one-to-one consent" rule, which would have required consent to name a single seller. That reduces one risk for businesses buying leads, but it doesn't loosen the baseline consent rules above. Revocation rules also changed in 2025, and carriers now police traffic on their own. Check current requirements with counsel before you build or rebuild a program.

## Mechanics that matter

- **Opt-out must work.** Honor STOP and other clear opt-out wording quickly and automatically. Confirm it once, then stop.
- **Disclosures.** Say who is texting, that messages are marketing, roughly how often, that message and data rates may apply, and link to your terms.
- **Quiet hours.** The federal rule bars calls and texts before 8 a.m. and after 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time. Some states are stricter.
- **Frequency caps.** Set a ceiling per week. Over-messaging causes opt-outs and complaints long before it causes lawsuits.
- **Carrier registration.** Business texting over standard 10-digit numbers requires 10DLC registration and follows CTIA guidelines. Unregistered traffic gets filtered or blocked.

## Transactional versus promotional

An order confirmation or appointment reminder is generally treated more loosely than a promotion, because it's tied to something the customer already asked for. Mixing them in one stream is a common, costly mistake. A single text that confirms an order and tacks on a coupon can turn the whole message promotional. Keep the streams separate and track them separately.

## Franchises and shared platforms

Consent collected under one entity's terms doesn't automatically cover a related but legally distinct business. Franchise groups and multi-brand companies should check that each location's opt-in language names the sender and would hold up if challenged. A shared platform won't solve this for you.

## Keep records

Platforms like Klaviyo, Attentive and Postscript build in a lot of compliance logic, but they can't fix a bad opt-in on your website. Liability traces back to how consent was collected. Keep a dated record of the exact opt-in wording shown at each point in time, with timestamps and the source of each opt-in. If a question comes up about a text sent eight months ago, you want to prove what consent language was live that day, not reconstruct it from old screenshots.

## Common mistakes we see

- **Buying or inheriting a phone list and texting it.** Consent doesn't transfer from a list seller to you.
- **Pre-checked opt-in boxes.** They undermine the idea of express consent. Leave the box unchecked.
- **Ignoring replies.** People answer texts with real questions. A reply that nobody reads is a missed sale and, sometimes, a complaint.
- **Sending from many numbers.** It makes consent records and opt-outs harder to manage, and carriers notice.
- **Treating a "no" as temporary.** If someone opts out, they are out until they opt back in themselves.

Each of these is cheap to fix and expensive to ignore. A short written SMS policy that names who owns consent, who answers replies and who handles opt-outs prevents most of them.

## The so-what

Compliance also helps performance. People who opted in clearly stay subscribed longer and engage more. This week, do three things: screenshot your live sign-up forms, confirm STOP works end to end, and check that your texting number is registered. Then put a quarterly reminder on the calendar to repeat the review with legal counsel.
