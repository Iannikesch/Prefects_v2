Growing an SMS list the wrong way produces a list that looks impressive on paper and performs terribly in practice, full of people who opted in under pressure, forgot they did, and now mark every message as spam the moment it arrives. Growing it the right way takes longer and produces smaller numbers at first, but the list that results actually engages, which is the entire point of building one in the first place.

## What not to do

The wrong way is more common than it should be: an aggressive pop-up demanding a phone number before a visitor can even see a website's homepage, a checkout flow that pre-checks an SMS opt-in box by default and buries the opt-out option in fine print, or a single-touch incentive so large, get 30% off just for texting us, that people opt in purely for the discount with zero intention of staying subscribed afterward. All three tactics inflate list size quickly. All three also produce a list with weak long-term engagement and a much higher opt-out rate once the initial incentive wears off.

## What works instead

Here's a hypothetical with made-up outcomes. A home goods retailer runs an aggressive pop-up offering 25% off for an SMS opt-in, appearing within three seconds of landing on the site regardless of browsing behavior. List size grows quickly, but within 60 days, a substantial share of that list has opted back out, and engagement on regular campaigns to the remaining subscribers sits well below the brand's email benchmark. A second retailer, selling a similar product, times its SMS opt-in prompt to appear only after a visitor has added an item to cart, offers a smaller, clearer incentive tied specifically to that purchase, and frames the ongoing value plainly, restock alerts, order updates, occasional exclusive early access, rather than promising nothing beyond the initial discount. That list grows more slowly but retains subscribers at a meaningfully higher rate over the same 60-day window.

## Consent comes first

Any SMS opt-in needs clear, unambiguous consent: a person actively checks a box or replies to confirm. A pre-checked default is generally treated as insufficient. The first message should say what they signed up for, how often to expect texts, and how to opt out at any time. In the U.S., consent rules such as the TCPA apply, and carriers enforce their own requirements too. Rules change, so check current requirements with counsel.

## Tie the prompt to value

Timing the opt-in prompt around genuine value, rather than a generic sitewide pop-up, tends to produce the strongest long-term list quality. A prompt tied to a specific, relevant moment, checkout, a restock notification signup on a sold-out product page, a post-purchase confirmation offering shipping updates via text, gives a person a concrete reason to opt in beyond a discount they'll forget about within a week. The subscribers who opt in for a specific, ongoing reason tend to stay subscribed longer than the ones who opted in purely to grab a one-time percentage off.

## Segment by how they joined

Segmenting new SMS subscribers by how they opted in, rather than treating the whole list identically from day one, lets a business calibrate expectations and message frequency to match why someone actually joined. A subscriber who opted in for restock alerts on one specific product probably doesn't want three unrelated promotional texts a week. A subscriber who opted in through a general sitewide incentive might tolerate more regular promotional content, provided it stays relevant to what they've shown interest in before.

## Consider double opt-in

Double opt-in, an extra confirmation step where a new subscriber replies to confirm before being added to the active list, is worth considering for businesses in higher-risk industries or anywhere compliance scrutiny runs higher than average. It adds modest friction and slightly slows list growth, but it produces a cleaner, more clearly consenting list and a stronger compliance record if that consent is ever questioned later.

## Honor the frequency you promised

Message frequency after opt-in matters just as much as how someone got onto the list in the first place. A subscriber who opted in expecting occasional restock alerts, then receives a daily promotional text within the first week, tends to opt back out quickly, regardless of how carefully the original opt-in was framed. Setting and honoring a clear frequency expectation, stated plainly at the point of opt-in, protects the list quality a careful acquisition strategy worked to build in the first place.

## The so-what

Careful opt-in practices produce smaller lists at first and stronger ones later. This week, review your current sign-up prompt: is the box unchecked, is the value clear, and does the first text state frequency and opt-out? Fix whichever is missing, then compare 60-day opt-out rates between your old and new opt-in points.
