Most seasonal campaigns start too late. A business notices that demand is rising, builds a promotion in a hurry, and launches into the peak already behind. The better approach is to plan backward from the date demand peaks and work out what has to be true at each earlier stage.

## Start from the peak date

Take an example. A garden center whose busiest stretch runs from the first week of April through mid-May. Call that the peak. The tempting plan is to launch ads on April 1. The backward plan asks what should be ready on that date: a larger email list, warmed-up audiences, inventory in stock, product pages updated, and staff scheduled.

## Eight to ten weeks out

Work back from there. Eight to ten weeks before the peak, the focus is list growth and audience building. Offer a spring planting guide in exchange for an email address. Collect SMS opt-ins with a clear promise, such as early access to new stock. Launch low-cost content and retargeting pools so there are people to market to when the season starts.

## Four to six weeks out

Four to six weeks out, shift to warming up. Send the planting guide series by email. Begin retargeting visitors who read the content. Test two or three offers on a small budget to find which one resonates, so the peak budget goes behind a proven message instead of a guess. This is the stage where most of the learning should happen, and cheaply.

With made-up numbers, the garden center starts list-building in early February and grows its email list from 4,100 to 5,600 by April 1. During the test phase, an offer built around a free planting consultation beats a 15% discount on cost per lead, $9 against $14. With that in hand, the center puts roughly 60% of its seasonal budget behind the consultation offer once the peak begins.

## During the peak

During the peak, the job is execution and watching. Daily budget checks, quick responses to inventory changes (don't advertise what's sold out), and fast replies to leads. Resist the urge to redesign anything. The test period was for experimenting. The peak is for running what works.

## Plan operations too

Operations deserve a place in the plan, since a marketing success can become an operational failure. If the campaign doubles inquiries and the team can only handle one and a half times the normal volume, the extra leads go cold and the reviews turn sour. Check inventory, staffing, and delivery capacity against the forecast well before launch, and agree on what happens if demand exceeds it.

## After the peak

After the peak ends, the work isn't done. Spend the following two weeks on follow-up: thank-you messages, review requests, and offers for customers who bought one product to buy a related one. Then run a debrief while memory is fresh. What did the budget produce? Which messages worked? What was missing from stock? Write it down, since next year's plan starts from these notes.

A few cautions. Demand curves shift. Weather, economic conditions, and competitor moves can push the peak by a week or two. Build flexibility into the calendar, and use last year's data as a guide, not a guarantee. And don't let the seasonal push drain the rest of the year. If a business cuts everything else to fund the peak, it may find the shoulder seasons weaker than they should be.

## Smaller teams and budgets

Smaller businesses can run a scaled-down version of the same plan. A single email sign-up offer in February, one test of two offers in March, and a focused budget in April is a seasonal campaign. It doesn't need a large team. It needs a calendar that starts early enough for the preparation to matter.

Creative and copy should be ready well before the launch date. Seasonal campaigns tend to run into production delays at the worst moment: photography that wasn't shot, product pages that need new descriptions, landing pages that need approval. Build a production calendar working back from the launch, and treat the final approval date as the real deadline, since everything downstream depends on it.

Budget should follow the same backward logic. Spending a small amount early on audience building, a moderate amount in the test phase, and the largest share in the peak is usually more efficient than a flat monthly figure. A common split puts something like 10% in the early phase, 20% in testing, and 70% in the peak, though the right split depends on how fast demand arrives in the business's category.

The owned channels carry much of the weight here. An email list grown in the quiet weeks before a season costs far less per contact than paid traffic during the season, when everyone is bidding on the same audience. The same list can be reused year after year, which means the early investment keeps paying back.

## The so-what

Pull up last year's sales by week and find your real peak. Count backward ten weeks and write down one thing to start on that date, even something small like adding an email sign-up offer to the website. That single date is the beginning of a plan.
