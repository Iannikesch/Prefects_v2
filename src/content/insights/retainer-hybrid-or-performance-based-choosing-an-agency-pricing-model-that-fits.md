A flat monthly retainer pays an agency the same amount whether a campaign performs brilliantly or falls flat. That's worth understanding before you sign, because the way an agency is paid shapes what it pays attention to.

## Three common structures

**Flat retainer.** A fixed monthly fee. It gives both sides predictability and suits long-term brand and strategy work, where outcomes are slow and hard to isolate.

**Performance-based.** Some or all of the fee is tied to results: cost per qualified lead, a share of attributed revenue, or a bonus for hitting agreed benchmarks.

**Hybrid.** A reduced base fee covers strategy and account management, plus a bonus tied to results. It splits the risk.

None of these is right for everyone. Plenty of agencies do strong work on retainers, driven by reputation and professional pride. The structure simply doesn't reward exceptional work any differently from average work, and over a long relationship that gap in incentives can show up in effort, attention or how quickly problems get raised.

## How a hybrid works in practice

Here's a made-up scenario. A regional home services company moves from a flat $8,000 monthly retainer to a hybrid: a $4,000 base covering strategy and account management, plus a bonus tied to cost per qualified lead staying under a pre-agreed threshold. Under the flat fee, optimization happened on a monthly cadence. Under the hybrid, the team checks performance weekly, because its compensation now moves with results. (Illustrative numbers.)

## When performance pricing fits

It requires reliable attribution first. A bonus tied to a number nobody trusts just creates a new argument about whose numbers are right. It also tends to suit direct-response and lead-generation work better than brand-building, where the link between activity and revenue is longer and harder to isolate.

## Design the bonus carefully

A bonus tied purely to lead volume can reward cheap, low-quality leads that hit the number and do little for revenue. Pair any volume goal with a close-rate or quality threshold so the incentive points at valuable leads, not just more of them. Agree on definitions, the data source and the measurement window in writing.

## Move in phases

Switching an existing relationship works better in steps than in one jump. Add a modest bonus layer on top of the current retainer for one quarter. If it works, reduce the base and raise the performance share. A full overnight switch asks an agency to take on a lot of uncertainty at once, which is a harder conversation than a gradual shift.

## What the conversation reveals

Asking about performance-linked terms is a useful way to learn how an agency views its own results. A thoughtful answer might include the limits of the model for your type of work, or a different way to share accountability. Evasion is worth noticing. Some work, such as brand strategy, still fits a fixed fee, so a "no" isn't automatically a red flag. Pay attention to the reasoning.

## An example

Take an example with made-up numbers. A company with a $6,000 monthly media budget compares three offers. A flat retainer of $3,000 covers a defined scope and doesn't change with results. A hybrid at $2,000 plus a bonus of 10% of attributed revenue above an agreed baseline gives the agency a share of growth. A pure performance model charges only on results but usually requires a higher percentage and a very clear definition of what counts.

Each can work. The retainer is predictable and favors work that takes time. The hybrid aligns incentives and adds some variability. The performance model shifts risk to the agency and tends to push the work toward whatever is easiest to count.

## Questions to ask before you sign

Ask who defines the baseline, how results are verified against your own records, what happens when a month is unusually good or bad and how either side can end the arrangement. If those answers are written down, the pricing model matters less than the clarity.

Whatever you choose, review the structure after a few months. Pricing models should fit the stage of the relationship, and an arrangement that made sense at launch may not suit a larger budget or a clearer set of results later. Agree up front on when you'll revisit it.

## The so-what

Decide what you want incentives to reward before you pick the structure. If you can measure customers and revenue reliably, ask for a hybrid with a clear quality gate. If you can't, a retainer with defined deliverables and quarterly goals is usually the better fit until your tracking improves.

Contract terms vary and this isn't legal advice. Have an attorney review any agreement before you sign.
