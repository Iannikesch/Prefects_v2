A flash sale ending in six hours sent exclusively through email is likely to reach a meaningful share of its audience only after the sale has already ended, simply because email doesn't get read on the same timeline text messages do. Choosing the right channel for a time-sensitive offer isn't a minor stylistic decision, it's often the difference between a promotion that actually reaches people while it's still relevant and one that technically got sent but rarely got seen in time.

## How quickly each gets read

The core difference between the two channels comes down to how quickly each one typically gets read. Text messages tend to be read within minutes of arriving, a habit built from years of texts being personal, immediate, and rarely batched for later reading the way email often is. Email, by contrast, sits in an inbox for hours or, for a lot of subscribers, days before it gets opened, if it gets opened at all, competing with dozens of other unread messages for attention whenever someone eventually gets around to checking.

## Use SMS for short shelf lives

That gap makes SMS the clear choice for anything with a short shelf life: a flash sale ending same-day, a limited-quantity restock likely to sell out within hours, a last-call reminder for an event starting that evening. Here's a made-up scenario with invented numbers. A specialty retailer runs a six-hour flash sale, sending the announcement through both email and SMS to overlapping segments of its list. Conversion from the SMS send happens overwhelmingly within the first ninety minutes after sending. Conversion from the email send trickles in far more slowly, with a meaningful share of email opens happening after the sale has already technically ended, representing pure wasted reach on a message that arrived too late to matter to the person reading it.

## Use email for context

Email still holds real advantages for offers that aren't time-sensitive, or that benefit from more context than a short text message can reasonably carry. A seasonal sale running for a full week, a detailed announcement about a new product line, anything that benefits from imagery, longer copy, or multiple calls to action, plays to email's strengths rather than SMS's. Cramming that same detailed announcement into a 160-character text message loses most of what made the offer compelling in the first place, and SMS's higher perceived intrusiveness means it should generally be reserved for messages that justify landing directly on someone's phone.

## Mind frequency and consent

Frequency tolerance differs sharply between the two channels as well, and it's worth factoring into the channel decision directly. A subscriber will tolerate a fairly high volume of email, several campaigns a week in some industries, without much complaint. That same subscriber's tolerance for SMS volume is considerably lower, and overusing the channel for offers that aren't urgent trains subscribers to ignore texts entirely, undermining the exact immediacy advantage that makes SMS valuable for the offers that actually warrant it.

Only text people who have clearly opted in, and follow your SMS provider's and carriers' rules. Consent requirements vary, so check with counsel.

## Hybrid and cost

A hybrid approach works well for offers that sit somewhere in between, neither a multi-week campaign nor a same-day flash sale. Leading with email to build initial awareness a day or two before an offer's deadline, then following with a single, well-timed SMS reminder closer to the actual cutoff, uses each channel for what it does best rather than forcing one channel to carry the entire promotion on its own.

Cost is a smaller but genuine factor in this decision too, worth acknowledging plainly. SMS carries a per-message cost that email typically doesn't, at least not in any way that scales directly with volume the way SMS billing usually does. That cost difference is rarely large enough to override a clear timing advantage for a urgent offer, but it's a reasonable factor to weigh when a message sits in the gray area between channels and either one would reasonably work.

## Test your own audience

Testing both channels against the same offer, when list overlap allows for a clean comparison, is worth doing at least once for any business uncertain where a specific type of promotion actually belongs. Comparing conversion timing and total conversion rate for an identical offer sent through each channel to comparable segments gives a business-specific answer rather than relying entirely on general industry patterns that may not map perfectly onto its own audience's particular habits.

Device and lifestyle habits shape this too, worth a brief mention since they explain some of the variation businesses see between industries. An audience skewing younger and more mobile-first tends to check text messages even more immediately than average, widening SMS's timing advantage further, while an audience with different phone habits, or a business-to-business audience checking email primarily during work hours, may see a smaller gap between the two channels than the general pattern described here would suggest.

## The so-what

Look at your next time-sensitive promotion, anything ending within 24 hours or built around limited quantity, and check whether it's currently planned as an email-only send. If so, that's a strong candidate for adding an SMS component specifically for the final hours of the offer, when speed of delivery matters more than anything else the message could say.
