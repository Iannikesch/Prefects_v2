Raising a price feels risky and lowering one feels generous, but most small businesses change prices by instinct and never learn what the change actually did. Revenue went up, or it didn't, and a dozen other things also happened that month. A structured pricing test is the way out of that fog.

## Test, don't guess

The goal of a test isn't conversion rate. It's revenue per visitor, or better, profit per visitor. A higher price almost always lowers conversion. The question is whether the extra margin on each sale more than makes up for the sales lost. A change that cuts conversions by 10% while raising price by 15% may be a win. Without measuring both, you can't tell.

## Measure revenue per visitor

Here's a hypothetical with made-up numbers. An online seller of specialty coffee subscriptions with a $28 monthly plan. The owner suspects the price is too low and wants to test $32. Instead of changing the price for everyone, the site shows $28 to half of new visitors and $32 to the other half, assigned at random, for six weeks. Existing subscribers are left alone.

## Assign prices at random

Random assignment matters. If the new price is shown only on weekends, or only to visitors from one ad campaign, other differences contaminate the result. Many testing tools handle random splits, but they need enough traffic. If the site gets 400 new visitors a month, a test won't yield a clear answer in six weeks. Smaller businesses may need to run longer or test bigger changes.

In this example, 1,150 visitors see the $28 price and produce 46 subscriptions, a conversion rate of 4.0%. Another 1,150 see $32 and produce 40 subscriptions, or 3.5%. Revenue per visitor on first-month pricing comes out at $1.12 versus $1.11. Essentially even. But the higher price carries a larger margin per subscriber, and if retention holds, it's the better choice.

## Look past the first sale

Then look past the first sale. Does the higher price affect cancellations or customer quality? Track retention for the two groups over several months. A price that attracts fewer but more committed customers may beat one that fills the funnel with people who leave in two months. This takes patience, which is why many tests get declared early and wrongly.

## Stay fair and transparent

Be careful about fairness and trust. Showing different prices to different people at the same time can annoy customers who notice, and in some contexts raises legal and reputational issues. Alternatives exist: test sequentially over comparable periods, test with new customers only, or test in separate markets. Choose the approach that suits the business and its customers, and check regulations that apply to your industry.

## Test the presentation too

Consider the other side of pricing, the presentation. The same $32 can feel different next to a $45 option than it does alone. Anchoring, bundling, and the order in which plans appear all shift how customers read a price. A test that changes the number only is simpler to interpret, but once the number is settled, a second test on how it's presented can be worth running.

Price tests tell you about price sensitivity for that product and audience in that period. They don't generalize forever. Competitors change, costs change, and customers' expectations drift. Treat a pricing test as a snapshot, and plan to revisit it every year or so.

Record the test properly. Write down the hypothesis, the dates, the groups, the results, and what was decided. A future owner or marketer should be able to read that note and understand what was learned. Pricing decisions made from memory tend to get relitigated every time someone new joins the discussion.

## Mind the sample size

Sample size deserves another word. Small differences in conversion rate are easy to mistake for real effects. If a test shows one price converting at 4.0% and another at 3.5%, a handful of extra sign-ups could swap the ranking. Decide in advance how many conversions each group needs, and be willing to say the test was inconclusive when it was. An inconclusive result is still information: it suggests the price change, in that range, isn't a large lever either way.

Not every price test is about the headline number. Testing a free trial against a money-back guarantee, or a monthly plan against an annual one, can change revenue as much as a price change does, with less customer backlash. Many businesses find their most valuable test isn't $28 versus $32 but how the offer is structured around the price.

Communicate with existing customers about changes. A price increase for current subscribers handled with notice and a clear reason lands very differently from a surprise charge. Offering a grace period or a locked-in rate for long-term customers costs some revenue and often buys goodwill that more than pays for itself.

## The so-what

A business that tests price once tends to keep testing, because the first result usually reveals how little it knew. Over several rounds, the pricing, packaging, and offers settle on numbers that reflect what customers actually do, not what the owner worried they might do, and the difference compounds across every sale made after that.

Showing different prices to different customers can raise legal and consumer-protection questions in some places. Confirm your approach with counsel before testing. This article is general information, not legal or compliance advice. Rules vary by location and change over time, so confirm current requirements with a qualified attorney before acting.
