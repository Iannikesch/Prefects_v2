Intent data is information that signals someone is actively researching a purchase. It comes from behavior like searches, content downloads and browsing, and it gets packaged into an alert: this person or company is showing buying signals right now.

The pitch is easy to like. Instead of marketing to everyone who might someday need your product, you focus on people who are in the market today. For B2B companies with long sales cycles and big deals, catching someone at the right moment can shorten the path from first contact to signed contract.

## Two kinds of intent signals

It helps to separate where the signal comes from.

- **First-party intent.** Behavior on your own channels: pricing-page visits, repeat visits, proposal opens, email clicks. You own it, it's specific to your brand, and it's usually the strongest signal you have.
- **Third-party intent.** Behavior collected across other sites and sold by a provider, such as research on topics related to what you sell.

Both are useful. They work best together: third-party signals help you find accounts that haven't met you yet, and first-party signals tell you which of them are warming up.

## Where it breaks down

Third-party intent has real limits. Signals are often aggregated and anonymized, so the "intent" you're buying might be three people at one company researching unrelated things. Quality also varies a lot between providers, and some of what's sold as intent data is firmographic guesswork with a more exciting name.

It tends to work well for B2B companies with a defined target account list and a sales team ready to act fast. It works poorly for low-cost consumer purchases, or for any business without a follow-up process. A signal that sits in a dashboard for a week is worth almost nothing.

## A pilot worth running

Before buying a subscription, test it.

Say you pick 300 target accounts and split them in two. On 150, your team gets intent alerts and follows up within a day. The other 150 get your normal outreach. After 90 days you compare meetings booked and pipeline created. If the alert group produced 14 meetings against 9 for the control group, you have evidence. If it produced 9 against 9, you've saved yourself an annual contract. (Illustrative numbers.)

Ask the provider these questions first:

1. How is the signal generated, and where does the data come from?
2. How fresh is it?
3. What share of flagged accounts have other clients converted, in a similar industry?

A provider confident in its data will answer with specifics. Smaller pilot packages scoped to a short window and a limited account list exist for this purpose, and they beat signing an annual deal on the strength of a pitch.

## Intent data isn't retargeting

The two get confused. Retargeting works from people who already visited your site, which is behavior you own. Intent data works from broader signals a third party collected, which is less certain and less owned, even when it's marketed with the same confidence.

## Plan for the follow-up

What happens after the signal arrives matters more than the signal. Intent data doesn't close deals. It tells you where to point your sales and marketing effort. Decide in advance what a flagged account gets: a specific email sequence, a call script, a retargeting campaign. Make sure someone has capacity to act. More signals without more follow-up capacity just builds a backlog of cold leads.

## What good follow-up looks like

Speed is the whole value of an intent signal. If a prospect visits your pricing page twice in a week and requests a proposal, a response within the same business day is a very different experience from one three days later. Set a service level before you buy anything: for example, every high-intent alert gets a personal email or call within four business hours.

Route alerts to the person who owns the account, include the history of what that person viewed, and track what happens. After a month you'll know which signals lead to meetings and which just create noise. Drop the noisy ones.

## The so-what

Intent data is worth paying for when you have a defined account list, a fast follow-up process and a way to measure the result. If you don't, spend first on your own signals. Start with who visits your pricing page, who opens proposals and which accounts return more than once. That costs little and shows you what your best prospects do before they buy. Once that works, test a third-party feed against a holdback group and keep it only if it beats your baseline.
