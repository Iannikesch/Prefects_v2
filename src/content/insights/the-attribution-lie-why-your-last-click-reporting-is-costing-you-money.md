Last-click attribution gives 100% of the credit for a sale to whatever the customer clicked right before converting. It's the default in a lot of reporting, it's easy to explain in a meeting, and it is often wrong.

## What it misses

Think about how people actually buy. Someone sees a Facebook ad and doesn't click. Two weeks later a retargeting ad shows up and they still don't click. A friend mentions the brand. They search the company name, click a paid ad for their own brand, and buy.

Last-click hands the whole win to that final branded search. The first ad and the retargeting spend get nothing.

## What it does to budgets

Here's a hypothetical. A business spends $10,000 a month across three channels and reports on last-click.

| Channel | Spend | Last-click sales |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Paid social | $4,000 | 12 |
| Display retargeting | $2,000 | 8 |
| Branded search | $4,000 | 80 |

Branded search looks like a hero, and paid social looks like a waste. So the owner cuts social. Three months later branded search volume drops, because fewer people are discovering the brand in the first place. The channel that was blamed had been feeding the one that got the credit. ## A fairer way to count

Multi-touch attribution spreads credit across the touchpoints in a customer's path. It takes more setup, because it needs ad platforms, website analytics and your CRM connected, so the full journey shows in one place instead of four dashboards that each tell a different story.

You can start small. A basic model that splits credit evenly across the first, middle and last touch is more honest than last-click alone. A rough multi-touch view beats a precise-looking number that misleads.

When you're ready for the next step, add an incrementality test: hold back a group that doesn't see a campaign and compare it to the group that does. The gap is the lift that campaign actually caused.

## Why last-click became the default

It's easy, not accurate. Each platform can report its own last-click conversions without talking to any other system. That convenience makes it feel rigorous. It comes with a number attached, so people trust it, but it is simply the easiest number to produce.

## The double-counting trap

Platforms also each claim credit for the same sale. Meta reports a purchase as a Meta win while Google Ads reports the same purchase as a search win. Add up every platform's reported conversions and you'll often find the total beats your actual sales, sometimes by a wide margin. That's a clear sign nobody has built a single source of truth.

## Long sales cycles are hit hardest

A homeowner sees an ad for a remodeling company, thinks about it for six weeks, gets a referral from a neighbor, and finally converts after a branded search. Last-click credits the search alone. The more touches between first exposure and purchase, the worse the distortion.

Last-click still has a job. It's useful for seeing which landing pages and offers close deals once someone is ready to buy. It just can't be the whole picture.

## A five-minute check you can run today

Add up the conversions every ad platform reports for last month. Then pull the real number of sales or leads from your CRM or point of sale. If the platform total is 30% or more above reality, you're double-counting, and your last-click numbers can't be trusted as they stand.

That gap doesn't mean anyone is lying. Each platform counts the conversions it touched, and many of the same customers were touched by several. It does mean no single platform's report should settle a budget question by itself.

## Talk about it internally, too

Sales teams sometimes push back when credit gets spread to marketing touches. Explain the model before you show results. When people understand why a channel earns partial credit instead of full or none, they argue less about the numbers and more about what to do next.

## Why this matters more for your biggest decisions

The cost of this distortion grows with the size of the decision. Trimming 5% from a small test campaign is cheap to reverse. Cutting a channel in half, or shifting a quarter's budget, is not. Before any large reallocation, check whether the channel being cut shows up earlier in customer paths, even when it rarely gets the final click. If it does, test the cut on a small slice first and watch what happens to the channel that appeared to be winning.

## The so-what

Ask your team or agency which attribution model your reporting uses. If the answer is "whatever the platform defaults to," you are probably underfunding the channels that build demand and overfunding the ones that harvest it. Ask to see how credit is assigned, then rerun last quarter's budget decision with that view and see whether you'd make the same call.
