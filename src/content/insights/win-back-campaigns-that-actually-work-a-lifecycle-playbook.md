Every customer list has a quiet graveyard of people who used to buy and stopped. Most brands send one generic "we miss you, here's 20% off" email to everyone who hasn't purchased in some arbitrary window. It rarely works well, because it treats very different customers as if they all left for the same reason.

This post is about lapsed buyers: people who purchased and then went quiet. If your problem is subscribers who stopped opening emails, see the post on re-engagement campaigns.

## Segment by why they left, not just how long

Someone who bought once and never returned had a different experience from someone who bought five times over two years and then drifted. Lumping them together wastes the chance to say something relevant to either.

**One-time buyers.** The goal is reintroduction. They never built a habit, so a discount alone won't create one. Remind them what the product does and why people love it. Think of it as a second welcome series.

**Previously loyal customers.** This person knows your brand and liked it. Something specific changed: a price increase, a product that disappeared, or a shift in their life. A direct, personal message works better than a discount-first one, sometimes with a simple "what happened? We'd like to know." It treats them as someone with a history.

## Time it in tiers

Triggering messages at 60, 90 and 180 days of inactivity, each worded for how lapsed the customer is, consistently outperforms one blast to the whole dormant list.

| Days inactive | Tone | Offer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60 | A nudge | New arrival or helpful reminder |
| 90 | More direct | Small incentive, tested |
| 180 | Last real attempt | Strongest offer you'll make |

After 180 days, move non-responders to a low-frequency list. Hitting a long-dormant contact with the same urgency as a 60-day nudge mostly produces unsubscribes and drags down deliverability for the rest of the list.

## Find the smallest incentive that works

A discount treats the problem as price. Often the problem was relevance, and no discount fixes irrelevance. Test offer types: a new product, a feature they haven't seen, free shipping, a small gift, a percentage off. Start low and raise it only if the smaller offer fails. Customers won back with a 10% offer are worth more than customers who needed 30%, and you protect margin on everyone else.

Predictive signals help too. If your data shows which customers are drifting before they hit 60 days, you can reach them earlier, when a light touch is enough.

## Test against a control group

Send the tiered sequence to half of the eligible lapsed list and the old single blast to the other half. Compare reactivation after 30 days.

Say 4,000 lapsed customers are split in two. The tiered group reactivates at 7.5%, which is 150 customers. The single blast group reactivates at 4.5%, which is 90. That 60-customer gap, multiplied by average order value, is your business case. (Illustrative numbers.) Single blasts tend to underperform tiered approaches by a wide margin once measured directly.

## What happens after they come back

A reactivated customer shouldn't fall back into the standard stream as if nothing happened. Send a short, warm acknowledgment, and watch their next few interactions closely. That raises the odds that this second chance turns into a lasting relationship rather than a second lapse.

Review win-back results once a year as their own topic, separate from the general marketing review. The lapsed pool shrinks and changes, so one quarter can mislead.

## The so-what

Win-back works when it's a lifecycle strategy, not an email template pulled out once a quarter. Most email and SMS platforms can build these tiers with basic segmentation. This month, split your lapsed customers into one-time and repeat buyers, write one message for each, and test a small incentive against no incentive. Keep the lower offer if the results match.
