Open almost any monthly marketing report and you'll find the same formula: green arrows, percentage increases and very little that tells you whether the business made more money. That isn't an accident. Reports get built to protect the relationship, and the easiest way to look good is to lead with metrics that almost always look decent.

## The tell is what gets top billing

Impressions, reach, engagement rate and click-through rate usually look healthy, because platforms are good at generating them. They're easy to grow and hard to argue with. They're also several steps away from revenue.

Metrics like revenue by channel, cost per customer and return on spend are harder to move and harder to hide behind. That's why they often appear on page nine, if at all.

## Same month, two summaries

Here's an illustration of one month told two ways.

**The impressive version:** "Impressions up 38%. Engagement up 22%. Click-through rate at 2.4%, above benchmark."

**The informative version:** "We spent $18,000 and produced 94 new customers at $191 each, down from $214 last month. Paid search is our best channel at $140 per customer. Paid social is at $310, so we cut it by $2,000 and moved it to search. We expect about 8 more customers next month. We're testing a new offer on landing page B."

Both are true. Only the second helps you decide something. (Illustrative numbers.)

## What a useful one-page report contains

Aim for a single page you can read in two minutes.

1. **The headline number.** Revenue or new customers this month, against last month and against target.
2. **Cost per customer, by channel.** The one table that guides budget.
3. **What changed and why.** Plain sentences, with a hypothesis for anything that moved.
4. **What didn't work.** Say it plainly, then say what happens next. Reports that only contain wins don't build trust.
5. **What we're testing.** One to three tests, each with a decision date.
6. **Decisions needed from you.** Budget moves, approvals, or data the agency needs.

Supporting detail belongs in an appendix. Anyone who wants to check the work can.

## Define the numbers once

Reports confuse people when "lead" or "conversion" means something different from one page to the next. Write definitions down once, at the start, and reuse them. If your agency's numbers don't match your CRM, ask for a reconciliation, not an apology. A small, explained gap is normal. An unexplained one is a problem.

## Pilot a new format for a quarter

If you change the reporting format, run it for a full quarter before dropping the old one. A single month isn't enough to tell whether a new structure surfaces better decisions or just looks different. Ask the agency to include the old metrics in the appendix during the pilot so nothing is lost.

## How to ask without starting a fight

You don't need to accuse anyone. Say something like: "I'd like the first page of each report to answer three questions: what did we spend, how many customers did it produce and what are we doing next month?" A good agency will agree, and one that resists may be telling you something.

## An example report, line by line

Consider a made-up case. A monthly report leads with 412,000 impressions, a 38% rise in social followers and a record number of video views. Those lines are accurate and none of them is a business result. On page four, in small type, sit 31 leads at $186 each, with 9 booked jobs.

A rewritten one-page version opens with the same month in a different order: spend, leads, booked jobs, cost per booked job and revenue, each compared with the target and the prior month. Impressions and followers move to a supporting section. Nothing was hidden in the original and nothing was added in the rewrite. The only change is what gets top billing, and that change decides what the room discusses.

A fair way to bring this up is to ask for the one-pager as a supplement for one quarter, then compare which version your team actually uses.

If your current report has been the same format for a year, change it before the next review. Habits form around whatever is easy to read, and a report built around revenue and booked jobs will quietly change what everyone discusses, including which numbers they ask about.

## The so-what

Next time a report lands in your inbox, skip to the end and see whether it answers the one question you care about: did this spending make the business more money? If it doesn't, raise it on your next call and ask for the one-page format above starting next month.
