A polished pitch deck, a confident presenter, and a handful of impressive-looking case study slides are what win most agency pitches, and none of those three things reliably predict whether the agency will actually do good work once the contract gets signed. The skills required to win new business and the skills required to execute a marketing campaign well overlap less than most businesses assume when they're sitting through a compelling first meeting.

## Who builds the pitch

Pitch presentations get built, understandably, by whoever's best at building pitch presentations, often a dedicated new business or sales team within a larger agency, rather than by the specific people who would actually manage a given account day to day. The strategic thinking presented in that meeting represents the agency's best, most polished thinking, produced with plenty of time and a highly motivated team focused entirely on winning that one deal. At some agencies, the account gets handed to a different team once it's signed, working across multiple clients simultaneously, with considerably less time to devote to any single relationship than the amount that went into the pitch that won it.

## Read case studies carefully

Case studies presented in a pitch deck deserve particular scrutiny, since they're selected specifically to showcase the agency's best possible results, not a representative sample of typical outcomes across the agency's full client roster. Here's a made-up scenario. A mid-size professional services firm evaluates three agencies, all presenting compelling case studies showing dramatic results for previous clients. What none of the pitches make clear is how those specific case study clients compare to the prospective client's own industry, budget, and starting point, details that matter enormously for whether a similar result is achievable versus simply an impressive but non-representative outlier the agency happens to showcase in every pitch it runs.

## Meet the real team

A more useful evaluation approach shifts attention away from the pitch itself and toward the specific people who would actually work on the account. Asking to meet the account manager and any specialist who'd handle the account's actual execution, not just the new business team presenting the pitch, gives a much clearer read on who'll actually be doing the work and how they think through a problem in real time, rather than relying entirely on a rehearsed presentation built by a different team specifically to win the deal.

References from current or recent clients, ideally clients in a similar industry or of a similar size and complexity to the prospective business, tend to reveal far more about ongoing execution quality than any pitch deck slide can. A reference conversation focused specifically on responsiveness, how problems get handled when something goes wrong, and whether the initial pitch team's promises actually matched the reality of working together day to day, surfaces the gap between winning a pitch and doing sustained, good work far more reliably than another round of case study slides ever could.

## Why the gap exists

Incentives explain part of this. Agencies are rewarded, through new client revenue, for winning pitches, and the skills that win pitches, polish, confidence, a strong narrative, get invested in accordingly. The skills that produce good ongoing execution, attentiveness, technical rigor, consistent communication, are rewarded more slowly and less visibly, through retention rather than a single high-stakes presentation, which means they don't always receive the same concentrated investment a pitch deck does.

## Try a small project

A short paid trial project, scoped narrowly enough to be reasonable for both sides, can reveal more about actual working style and execution quality than an extended pitch process ever will, since it involves the real team doing real work against a real, if smaller, deliverable, rather than a rehearsed presentation built specifically to impress. Not every agency will agree to this structure, but the ones willing to prove themselves on a smaller scope before asking for a full commitment are often signaling genuine confidence in their actual execution, not just their ability to present well.

The initial discovery process an agency runs before a pitch is itself worth evaluating carefully, since it often previews how thoroughly that agency will actually engage with a business's specific situation once the relationship is underway. An agency that asks detailed, business-specific questions before presenting anything, rather than arriving with a largely templated deck adapted only superficially to the prospective client, tends to carry that same level of specific attention into the ongoing account relationship as well.

Timeline pressure during the pitch process itself is worth watching too, since it often reveals something about how the relationship will be managed afterward. An agency pushing hard for a fast decision, framed as a limited-time offer or a rapidly closing capacity window, is applying a sales tactic that has little to do with actual account fit, and a business that feels rushed into signing rarely has the chance to properly evaluate the specific team and references that matter far more than any pitch deck slide.

## The so-what

Ask one question during any agency evaluation: who, by name and role, will work on this account day to day, and can I talk to them before I sign? A clear, fast answer is a good sign. Hesitation is worth paying attention to.
