A business spends $6,000 a month on ads and sends every click to a landing page that takes five seconds to load. Some visitors leave before it finishes. Those clicks were paid for, and nothing came of them. That's the obvious cost of a slow page. There's a less obvious one: on some platforms, a poor landing page experience can raise what you pay per click in the first place.

## You pay twice

In Google Ads, landing page experience is one of the components that feeds into Quality Score, which in turn influences ad rank and the price paid for a click. A page that loads slowly, isn't relevant to the ad, or is hard to use on a phone can drag that component down. The effect isn't dramatic in every account, and Google doesn't publish an exact formula, but the direction is consistent: better pages tend to earn cheaper clicks.

Speed matters on the human side too. People on mobile connections are impatient and distracted. If the page stalls, they go back to the results and click someone else. Each added second of delay tends to cost conversions, though the exact rate varies by site and audience, which is precisely why it's worth measuring on your own pages instead of relying on a quoted industry figure.

## Measure it

Measuring is free. Google's PageSpeed Insights tool reports how a page performs on mobile and desktop, including metrics tied to how quickly the main content appears and how stable the layout is while loading. Run your highest-traffic landing pages through it. Look at the mobile score first, since for many businesses a large share of ad clicks come from phones, often on connections slower than the office Wi-Fi a developer tested on.

## Common culprits

The usual culprits are boringly familiar. Oversized images, often straight from a phone or a designer, sometimes several megabytes each. Too many third-party scripts, such as chat widgets, heatmaps, and old tracking tags nobody removed. Heavy page builders carrying a lot of unused code. Slow hosting. Fixing the images alone often produces the biggest gain for the least effort.

## An example

Here's a made-up scenario with invented numbers. A roofing company whose main landing page takes 4.8 seconds to load on mobile because the hero image is 6 MB. A developer compresses the image, removes two unused scripts, and moves the page to faster hosting. Load time drops to 1.9 seconds. Over the next six weeks, the page's conversion rate climbs from 3.1% to 4.2%, and cost per lead falls by about a quarter on the same ad spend. Part of that is more visitors sticking around, and part may be better click prices, but the owner doesn't need to separate the two to see that the work paid off.

## Match the message

Relevance is the other half. A visitor who clicked an ad about emergency water heater repair and lands on a generic homepage has to hunt for what they came for. A page that continues the promise of the ad, with the same headline language, a clear next step, and a phone number that's easy to tap, performs better and tends to rate better with platforms. Message match is cheap to improve and easy to neglect.

## Test the whole path

Don't forget the form or checkout itself. A fast page followed by a clunky form wastes the gain. Test the whole path on an actual phone, on cellular data, not office Wi-Fi. Tap every button. Fill in every field with a thumb. The experience your customers have is the one that counts, and it's often noticeably worse than the one the team sees on a laptop.

Page weight accumulates quietly. Every new marketing tool adds a script, and nobody removes the old ones when a tool gets replaced. A page that was fast at launch can be slow two years later with no single change to blame. Schedule a review every few months, and ask one question of every script on the page: what would we lose if this were gone? If nobody can answer, it's a candidate for removal.

Layout choices on mobile matter beyond raw speed. A phone number that can't be tapped, a form that requires pinching and zooming, or a banner that covers the main button all cost conversions in ways speed tools won't catch. A sticky call button or a short form near the top of the page often helps more than another round of image compression.

When making changes, resist the urge to fix everything at once. Compress the images first, remeasure, then address scripts, then hosting. Changing five things in a weekend makes it impossible to know which one helped, and occasionally one of the fixes breaks something else, such as a tracking tag that got removed along with a script that looked unused. Verify that conversion tracking still fires after every change to the page.

Google Ads reports landing page experience at the keyword level as below average, average, or above average. If your most expensive keywords carry a below-average rating, start there, since improvements on those pages pay back fastest. It's a cheap way to decide which page deserves the first afternoon of work.

## The so-what

This week, load your main ad landing page on your phone over cellular data and time it. Then run it through PageSpeed Insights. Compress the images first, remeasure, and confirm your conversion tracking still fires after every change.
