A pitch often mentions the senior team that will be on the account. Months later, the account can end up with a different team after a promotion, a move or a change in workload. Nobody flags it as a problem exactly. It quietly resets the clock on everything the previous account manager had learned about the business.

## What turnover really costs

Team changes can happen in any agency relationship, and they're easy to overlook when choosing a partner. Every time a new person picks up an account, there's a relearning period, sometimes weeks and sometimes longer, where knowledge about what's already been tried, what didn't work and why can get lost.

The cost isn't obvious on an invoice. It shows up as strategy that seems to reset every few months, campaigns that repeat mistakes already made and fixed a year earlier, and a client who finds themselves re-explaining the same business context to a third or fourth person in eighteen months. A composite example: a mid-size B2B company works with an agency for three years and goes through five different account managers in that span. Each new manager, understandably, wants to make their own mark, sometimes rebuilding campaign structures that were already working, not because the old structure was broken, but because the new person didn't know why it existed and defaulted to rebuilding it their own way instead.

## Why it happens

Staffing changes can happen for ordinary reasons: workloads shift, people are promoted or someone moves on. Whether the account keeps continuity depends on how the agency plans for it, which is exactly why it's worth asking about.

Account size can also shape who works on an account and how long they stay. That isn't a universal rule, and some agencies protect continuity well, so it's better to ask than to assume.

## How to test for it before you sign

There's a way to test for this before signing a contract, not just after. Asking a prospective agency directly how long their average account manager stays on a single account, and asking for a specific number rather than a vague reassurance, tends to surface useful information fast. An agency confident in its retention will usually answer with a real figure. One that deflects the question, or answers only in generalities about a great team culture, is telling a business something worth hearing before signing anything.

## The one lever you control

Documentation is the one lever a client actually controls here, and it's underused. Requiring a shared, living strategy document, one the client has access to independent of the agency's internal tools, that captures what's been tried, what worked, what didn't, and why current decisions were made, means a handoff doesn't have to start from zero. It isn't glamorous work, but a documented account survives a staffing change. An undocumented one restarts from scratch every single time, and the client pays for that restart in slower results and repeated mistakes, whether or not it ever shows up as a separate line item.

## In-house teams have the problem too

In-house teams face the same problem. A marketing hire who leaves after a year takes undocumented knowledge with them too. The fix in both cases is documentation, and the questions below apply whether the work is done in-house or with an agency.

## The so-what

Ask your current agency directly: how many people have touched your account in the past two years, and where's the documentation that would let a brand-new person pick it up tomorrow without starting over? If nobody can answer that clearly, that gap is probably costing more than the invoice suggests. It's a fair question to ask, and a good agency will have a real answer ready rather than a reason it's complicated.
