A local service business in Google's ecosystem has two main paid options that look similar and work very differently. Traditional search ads charge by the click and let the business control keywords, copy, and landing pages. Local Services Ads charge by the lead and show up in a distinct box at the top of the results, with a trust badge. Which one deserves the budget depends on how each performs for a specific business.

## Two different models

Local Services Ads are available for certain categories and areas, and they require verification. Google typically checks licenses, insurance, and background information for the business, and then displays a trust badge. That screening is a real selling point for customers choosing a plumber or a lawyer from a page of strangers, and it's a requirement businesses have to meet before they appear.

The pricing model is the biggest difference. In search ads, you pay when someone clicks, whether or not they call or buy. In Local Services Ads, you pay when a customer contacts you through the ad, usually by phone or message, and the price per lead varies by category and market. Invalid or irrelevant leads can often be disputed for credit, though the process takes effort and the rules change, so check what's currently allowed.

## Control and ranking

Control differs too. With search ads, you choose the keywords, write the ad copy, send people to a page you designed, and use negative keywords to filter out waste. With Local Services Ads, Google decides much of that. You set budget, service area, hours, and job types, but you can't write the ad or choose the landing page. The ad is built from your profile, your reviews, and your responsiveness.

Ranking in Local Services Ads leans heavily on factors a business can influence but not buy outright: review count and rating, how quickly and reliably you respond, proximity to the customer, and the completeness of your profile. A business that answers every call and has strong reviews can outrank a competitor that spends more. The channel rewards operational quality, which suits good operators and punishes sloppy ones.

## An example

Here's a hypothetical with made-up numbers. A plumbing company that puts $3,000 a month into each channel for a quarter. The Local Services Ads produce 38 leads at about $79 each, and 11 become booked jobs, a cost of about $273 per job. The search campaign produces 21 leads at about $143, and 10 become booked jobs, or $300 per job. On cost per lead, Local Services Ads look nearly twice as good. On cost per booked job, the gap shrinks to around 9%, because the search leads were more qualified.

That illustrates a general rule: compare the channels on cost per booked job or revenue, not cost per lead. Leads from different channels aren't equivalent. A lead from a screened, pay-per-lead box might include price shoppers and out-of-area inquiries, while a click on a well-targeted search ad may come from someone with a specific problem. The funnel work described earlier in this series applies equally here.

## Handle calls and track them

Call handling is critical for Local Services Ads, since responsiveness feeds the ranking and since you pay for the lead whether or not you answer. Missed calls waste money twice: the lead fee is spent and the customer calls a competitor. Make sure someone answers during your listed hours, and consider an answering service for peak times.

Tracking is another difference. Local Services Ads provide their own lead reporting, including call recordings in many cases, but the data lives in Google's interface and may not flow into your CRM automatically. Decide how you'll record which booked jobs came from which channel, or the comparison above will be impossible to run.

## Use both

The two channels can also complement each other. Local Services Ads capture people who are ready to call and trust a screened badge. Search ads can target specific services, such as water heater installation, with tailored messaging and landing pages. Running both lets you learn where each is strongest, and you can shift budget as the data comes in.

Availability and rules vary by category and location, and Google changes the program over time, so check what's offered in your market and how the current terms read. Treat any specifics in this post as a starting point, not a substitute for the platform's own documentation.

Reputation work pays double here. The same reviews that help Local Services Ads rankings also feed your regular local listings and your website's credibility, so effort spent collecting them isn't tied to one channel. It's one of the few investments that improves results in both of the options discussed here.

Start with a test, not a switch. Run both channels for a defined period with budgets large enough to produce at least a few dozen leads each, and compare cost per booked job at the end. Moving everything to one channel on the strength of a single month's lead cost is how businesses end up optimizing the wrong number.

## The so-what

Test both channels for a defined period, track which booked jobs came from which, and compare cost per booked job. Whatever you choose, build the foundations that serve either one: strong reviews, fast call response and accurate tracking.
