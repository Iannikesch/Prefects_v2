An average can be true and useless at the same time. A subscription business reports that 58% of customers are still active after six months. That sounds fine. But if customers acquired in one month stay at 41% and customers acquired in another stay at 71%, the average is hiding the single most important fact about the business: some customers are worth far more than others, and you can see which ones if you look.

## Averages mislead

Cohort analysis is the habit of grouping customers by when (or how) they arrived and then following each group over time. A cohort might be everyone who bought for the first time in January. Or everyone who came from a particular campaign. Instead of one blended number, you get a set of comparable stories.

The basic table is simple. Rows are cohorts, say one for each month of first purchase. Columns are time since that first purchase: month one, month two, month three. Each cell shows something you care about, such as the percentage still active or the revenue per customer so far. Any spreadsheet can build one in an hour, and the pattern usually jumps out.

## An example

Here's a hypothetical with made-up numbers. A meal kit company reviewing 12 monthly cohorts. The blended six-month retention is 58%. Looking by cohort, the customers acquired during a deep-discount promotion in March retain at 41% after six months, while the cohort that arrived through referrals in May retains at 71%. The promotion filled the funnel. It didn't fill it with people who stayed. Without the cohort view, the company might have run the promotion again because it produced the most new customers.

## Judge by what happens next

That's the heart of it. Cohorts let you judge acquisition by what customers do after they arrive, not just by how many arrive. A campaign with a cheap cost per customer and poor retention can be more expensive in practice than one that costs more up front and produces loyal buyers. This links directly to lifetime value, since cohorts show you how LTV is really building up.

They also reveal whether the business is improving. If each newer cohort retains better than the one before it, product changes, onboarding improvements, or better targeting are working. If newer cohorts do worse, something has quietly deteriorated, and a blended average will hide it for months because older, healthier cohorts prop the number up.

## Read cohorts carefully

A few cautions. Cohorts can be small, and small cohorts are noisy. A group of 40 customers can swing wildly on a couple of cancellations, so don't over-read tiny groups. Seasonality can also distort comparisons: customers acquired in December behave differently from those acquired in July for reasons that have nothing to do with campaign quality. Compare like with like when you can.

Recent cohorts are incomplete. A cohort that started last month hasn't had time to show six-month retention, and that empty cell isn't a zero. Many first attempts at cohort tables go wrong by comparing a mature cohort's long history to a young cohort's short one. Line them up by the same elapsed time so the comparison is fair.

## Slice by more than date

You can slice cohorts by more than date. Group by acquisition channel, by first product bought, by the offer used, by geography, by whether the customer opted into SMS. Each cut answers a different question. Does Meta bring in customers who last as long as search customers? Do people who start with the premium plan behave differently from those who start with the basic one?

Retail and ecommerce businesses can use the same approach with repeat purchase instead of subscriptions. What share of January's first-time buyers placed a second order within 90 days? Did that share differ for customers who came through email versus paid ads? The second purchase is often the moment a one-time buyer becomes a customer, and cohorts show how often it happens.

Share the findings in plain terms. A chart with cohort lines drifting up or down tells a story that executives grasp quickly. The conversation moves from 'how many customers did we get' to 'what happened to them,' which is the better question.

## Start with what you have

Start with whatever data you already have. A basic export from your store or CRM with customer ID, first order date, and order dates is enough to build a first cohort table. Don't wait for a perfect analytics setup. A rough version built this week teaches more than a polished one built next year.

Use cohorts to evaluate campaigns after the fact. Tag the acquisition source for each customer, then compare the cohorts from a given campaign with the ones before and after. If a promotion brought in customers who churned at twice the usual rate, the true cost of the promotion includes the lifetime value it failed to deliver. That reframing often changes which campaigns get repeated.

## The so-what

Export customer ID, first order date and order dates from your store or CRM this week and build one cohort table. Compare the lines. If a few of them stand out, find out what those customers had in common.
