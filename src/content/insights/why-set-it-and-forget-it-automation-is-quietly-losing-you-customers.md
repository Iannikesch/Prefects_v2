Marketing automation earned its reputation as a time-saver, and it is one. The trouble starts when a business treats the initial setup as the finish line instead of the starting point. A welcome flow built two years ago, a cart abandonment sequence written before the last product line launched, an SMS win-back campaign nobody has touched since the platform migration. All of it still fires on schedule. None of it necessarily reflects the business as it actually exists today.

## Failure that makes no noise

The failure mode here is quiet, which is exactly why it's dangerous. A broken automation doesn't send an error message. It just keeps running, sending a discount code for a product that got discontinued eight months ago, or welcoming a new subscriber with messaging that references a promotion that ended last spring. Everything looks like it's working, because emails and texts are still going out on schedule. Whether they still make sense is a different question entirely, and it's one nobody's checking unless someone deliberately builds in the habit of reviewing.

## What it looks like

A composite example: an ecommerce brand builds a five-email welcome flow when it launches, timed around a specific seasonal promotion running at the time. Eighteen months later, that promotion is long gone, but the flow still references it in email three, offering a discount code that no longer exists. New subscribers hit a dead link or an expired offer, and some percentage of them simply unsubscribe or mark the message as spam, quietly damaging sender reputation in the process, all from a flow nobody's opened in over a year because it was technically still working.

## Review on a schedule

The businesses that avoid this don't necessarily rebuild automations constantly. They schedule a review, quarterly is common, sometimes twice a year for a smaller list, where someone actually reads through every active flow from the customer's point of view, not just checks that it's technically still sending. Does the offer referenced still exist? Does the timing still match how the business currently operates? Does the tone still match the brand's current voice, especially if messaging or positioning has shifted since the flow was written?

Use this checklist for each flow:

- Does every offer, link and product it mentions still exist?

- Does the timing match how the business runs today?

- Is the audience definition still current?

- Does the tone match the brand now?

- Is anyone reading the replies?

Segmentation logic decays the same way content does, and often gets missed because it's less visible in a quick glance through email copy. A flow built around a customer segment defined two years ago, before a product line expanded or a new customer type became the majority of the business, might be routing people into sequences that made sense for the business as it existed then, not as it exists now. Nobody notices because the automation is still technically firing correctly. It's just increasingly firing at the wrong people, or with the wrong message for who those people have become.

There's a specific failure pattern worth naming: automations that were built around a promotion or a product that no longer exists tend to survive the longest precisely because nobody's watching for them. A flow tied to something currently running gets noticed quickly if it breaks, since someone's actively paying attention to that promotion elsewhere. A flow quietly referencing something that ended over a year ago has no one checking on it at all, which is exactly why these are often the ones doing the most damage by the time somebody finally looks.

## Migrations hide gaps

Platform migrations make this worse rather than better, somewhat counterintuitively. Moving from one email or SMS platform to another usually means someone manually rebuilding every flow in the new system, and it's common for a handful of older, less obviously important automations to get skipped in that rebuild, either because nobody remembered they existed or because they seemed too minor to prioritize during a migration already running behind schedule. Those gaps rarely get discovered through a deliberate audit. They get discovered when a customer complains about a message that no longer makes sense.

## Give it an owner

Ownership matters as much as the review schedule itself. Automation that belongs to everyone on a marketing team in theory tends to belong to no one in practice, since each person assumes someone else is keeping an eye on it. Assigning a specific person, even on a small team, as the one responsible for reviewing active flows on a set schedule turns a vague intention into an actual task that happens, rather than a good habit that quietly never gets around to itself. It's a small structural fix, and it tends to catch the problem long before a customer ever notices, which is exactly the point of checking on a schedule instead of waiting for a complaint to force the issue.

## The so-what

Automation reduces manual work. It doesn't reduce the need for oversight. Put a quarterly review on the calendar this week, name one person who owns it, and start with your oldest flow. If you find a dead offer, fix it today and check how many people received it.
