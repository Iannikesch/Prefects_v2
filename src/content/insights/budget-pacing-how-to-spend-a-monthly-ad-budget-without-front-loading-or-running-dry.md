A business sets a $10,000 monthly ad budget and checks in on the 20th. It has spent $8,900. The rest of the month will be quiet, or the owner will need to decide whether to add money. Either way, the budget wasn't managed. It was just spent.

## Spending isn't managing

Pacing is the discipline of spreading spend across the month so that it lands where results are likeliest. It's not the same as dividing by 30. Demand isn't flat. A restaurant's best days run Thursday through Saturday. A tax preparer's best weeks come in March. An HVAC company's budget should flex with heat waves. A flat daily budget treats every day as equal, and they aren't.

Platforms add their own wrinkles. Google Ads, for example, can spend up to twice a campaign's average daily budget on a given day, as long as the monthly total stays within the limit. That's by design, since it lets the system capture high-demand days. But it also means daily spend can look wildly uneven, and a business that doesn't expect it may assume something is broken when nothing is.

## Find your best days

The first pacing question is where the month's most valuable days fall. Look at past data. Which days of the week, and which weeks of the month, produce the most conversions per dollar? Many businesses find their best performance concentrated in a smaller slice of the calendar than they assumed. Budget should lean toward those periods, within reason, instead of being spread evenly over days that rarely produce anything.

## Leave a reserve

The second question is how much room to leave. Holding back 10% to 15% of the monthly budget for the final week gives flexibility to respond to something unexpected, such as a competitor going quiet, a sudden surge in demand, or a promotion that outperforms. Spending everything by the 20th removes the option to respond, and the last third of the month goes dark at the worst possible time.

## An example

Here's a hypothetical with made-up numbers. A landscaping company with a $4,000 spring budget. In the first version, it runs a flat daily budget and exhausts the money by April 22, just as a warm stretch drives a surge of searches. Competitors capture the leads. In the second version, the company starts the month at a lower daily level, reserves $600 for the final week, and raises bids on the two weekends with the best forecasts. Same total spend, but more of it lands on the days when homeowners are actually calling.

## Watch both directions

Watch for the reverse problem as well: underspending. A campaign that limits itself by targeting too narrowly, bidding too low, or sitting in ad review may finish the month with money unspent. Unspent budget isn't savings if it means leads you could have won went elsewhere. Check mid-month whether spend is on track, and look for the cause if it isn't.

A simple weekly rhythm helps. Every Monday, compare month-to-date spend with the plan. Note any day that spent far above or below average and ask why. Adjust daily budgets or bids by modest amounts rather than dramatic swings, which can reset the learning phase in automated campaigns and cause their own instability.

There's a late-month temptation worth naming: the scramble to use up whatever is left. Pushing money out in the last two days because the budget is 'use it or lose it' usually buys the weakest clicks of the month. If the money can't be deployed efficiently, let it roll into next month's plan or hold it for a better week. Spending for the sake of an empty balance isn't a strategy.

## Pace each campaign on its own

When several campaigns share one total budget, pace each according to its own demand curve. A branded search campaign might need steady daily spend, while a seasonal promotion needs most of its money in a short window. Treating them identically, with the same daily split, tends to starve the campaign with the best near-term opportunity. Write down the intended split before the month begins so changes are deliberate.

The platform's own data helps with planning. Most ad platforms show hourly and day-of-week performance reports. Pull three months of data and look at cost per conversion by day. A pattern that repeats for twelve weeks isn't a coincidence, and it's more reliable than intuition about which days are busy. Use it to set bid adjustments or daily budget multipliers, and revisit when the season turns.

Account for pending conversions, too. Many businesses judge pacing by the conversions recorded so far, but a lead generated on the 28th may not close until next month, and platforms often report conversions with a delay. Comparing an incomplete month with a complete one makes late-month spending look worse than it is. When reviewing, use cost per lead as it stands, and look at revenue only for periods old enough to have matured.

Communicate the plan to whoever approves the money. An owner who sees $2,100 spent in one day without context may panic and cut the campaign. A short note at the start of the month explaining the planned pace, and the reason for the heavier weekends, avoids a lot of unnecessary phone calls and keeps the account from being yanked around by anxiety.

## The so-what

Pacing comes down to deciding in advance where the money should go and checking weekly that it's going there. A budget spent evenly is easy to run, but a budget that follows demand usually performs better, and a few minutes of calendar planning at the start of the month is what makes the difference.
