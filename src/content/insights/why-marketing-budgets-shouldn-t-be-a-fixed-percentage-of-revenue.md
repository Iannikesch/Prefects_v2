Setting the marketing budget as a percentage of revenue is one of the most common practices in small business, and it has an appealing simplicity. Pick a number, often somewhere in a commonly quoted range for your industry, multiply it by last year's revenue, and you're done. The trouble is that it answers a question nobody should be asking.

## What the percentage can't tell you

The question it answers is 'what do businesses like ours usually spend?' The question that matters is 'how much can we profitably spend to get more customers?' Those two have different answers, because the second depends on your margins, your sales process, your capacity, and how well each dollar actually works. A percentage ignores all of them.

It also behaves badly in both directions. When revenue falls, the budget shrinks automatically, just when more marketing might be needed to recover. When revenue spikes, the budget balloons, whether or not the business can absorb more customers or the channels can use the money well. The rule moves with last year's results rather than with this year's opportunity.

## Start with unit economics

The better starting point is unit economics. How much is a customer worth, and how much can you spend to acquire one while meeting your payback target? If the answer is $150 per customer and you want 400 new customers next year, the arithmetic suggests a budget of about $60,000. That's the figure the business case supports, regardless of what percentage of revenue it happens to be.

## An example

Here's a hypothetical with made-up numbers. A home services company with $2.4 million in revenue that follows an 8% rule. Last year's record revenue pushes the rule's budget up by $60,000. But its unit economics say that adding a booked job costs about $210 at the margin, and the crew can handle roughly 90 more jobs a year before quality suffers. That supports about $19,000 of additional spend. The percentage rule would add three times that, much of it chasing demand the crew can't serve.

The opposite mistake is just as common. A business with great unit economics, say a customer worth $900 that costs $200 to acquire, may be underinvesting badly if it sticks to a 6% rule. The math says to keep spending until the marginal customer stops paying back, and that point may be far beyond the percentage's ceiling. Growth gets capped by a rule of thumb.

## Marginal returns, definitions and limits

Marginal returns complicate things. Average cost per customer isn't the same as the cost of the next customer. Early dollars in a channel tend to be efficient, and later dollars less so. A sound budget identifies where the marginal dollar still earns more than it costs, and stops short of where it doesn't. Testing increments, rather than assuming the average holds, shows where that line is.

Define what counts. Does the budget include agency fees, software, creative production, and staff time, or only media spend? Two businesses can quote the same percentage while counting completely different things, which makes comparisons to published benchmarks unreliable. Decide on a definition, write it down, and stick with it from year to year.

Capacity is a hard limit that percentages ignore. More leads don't help a business that can't serve them, and a flood of inquiries that goes unanswered damages reputation. Tie the budget to how many customers the operation can handle, and revisit it as capacity changes with hiring, equipment, or new locations.

Cash flow matters too. A business with thin reserves may not be able to wait for a 12-month payback, however attractive the lifetime numbers look. Set the budget so that the payback period fits the cash position, and treat any spending that stretches beyond that as a deliberate bet instead of a routine expense.

## Use the percentage as a smoke alarm

Percentages aren't useless. They work as a sanity check. If the unit-economics budget comes out at 2% of revenue or 30%, it's worth asking whether the assumptions are sound, since that is far outside what similar businesses do. Use the percentage as a smoke alarm, not as the plan.

## Review, range, refresh

Review the budget more than once a year. Quarterly checks against actual acquisition costs, capacity, and cash let the business raise spending when the economics are strong and pull back when they're not. A budget that adapts to evidence, within guardrails, will usually outperform one fixed in January.

Use a range, not a single number. Instead of one figure, set a base budget that fits the plan and a stretch amount you'd release if results are strong. Releasing money in stages against evidence keeps spending tied to performance instead of to a number set months before anything was learned.

Revisit the assumptions that feed the math. A customer value built on last year's retention and margin goes stale as costs and behavior shift. Update the inputs at least annually, and note when a change in pricing, supplier costs, or product mix has altered what a customer is worth.

## The so-what

Work out three numbers this week: what a customer is worth in profit, what you're willing to spend to get one, and how many more customers you could serve. Multiply the last two to get a first-draft budget, then set it beside your current percentage-based figure and look hard at the difference.
